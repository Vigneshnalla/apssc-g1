## 1. Meaning of marriage

**Marriage** is a socially recognised relationship between individuals that establishes certain rights, duties and kinship relationships. It may be recognised through custom, religion, civil law or a combination of these.

### Marriage as a social institution
A **social institution** is an established system of norms, roles and rules that organises an important area of social life. Marriage is a social institution because societies develop rules about partner selection, ceremonies, family responsibilities, inheritance and separation.

### Importance of marriage
- It is one common way of forming a family.
- It creates relationships between spouses and their families (in-laws).
- It can organise shared economic responsibilities and care.
- It may provide a setting for raising and socialising children.
- It is regulated by customs and laws.

**Remember:** Marriage is common across societies, but its form differs. It is not correct to say that every family must begin through marriage, or that every marriage is permanent.

## 3. Characteristics of marriage

1. **Social recognition:** It is recognised by social custom, religious ceremony, civil procedure or law.
2. **Rules and norms:** Societies regulate who may marry whom and how a marriage is formed.
3. **Rights and duties:** Marriage may create legal duties and rights relating to support, property, inheritance and children.
4. **Kinship:** It creates affinal relationships, such as relationships with a spouse’s family.
5. **Cultural variation:** Marriage practices differ between societies, communities and historical periods.
6. **Relative continuity:** Marriage is usually intended to continue, but may end through divorce or death.
7. **Family and care:** It may organise shared living, economic cooperation and caregiving.
8. **Legal regulation:** Laws may cover valid marriage, prohibited relationships, bigamy, divorce and maintenance.

**Avoid overgeneralisation:** Marriage is not identical everywhere, and not all families or child-rearing arrangements are based on marriage.

## 4. Functions of marriage

### A. Social functions
- Creates recognised family and kinship relationships.
- Connects the relatives of spouses.
- Helps transmit customs, values and traditions.
- May provide a setting for children’s socialisation.

### B. Economic functions
- Partners may share income, property and household work.
- Family members may cooperate in housing, food and care.
- Marriage may affect property and inheritance, according to applicable law.

### C. Emotional and care functions
- May provide companionship, emotional support and mutual care.
- May organise care for children and other family members.

### D. Regulatory functions
- Societies define permitted and prohibited relationships.
- Laws establish conditions for valid marriage and rules about divorce, maintenance and other issues.

**Balanced conclusion:** These are possible functions, not guaranteed outcomes of every marriage. Families also exist outside marriage.

## 7. Forms of marriage

### A. Monogamy
**Monogamy** means having one spouse at a time.

- **Non-serial monogamy:** one marriage, with no subsequent remarriage after it ends.
- **Serial monogamy:** a person may remarry after divorce or the death of a spouse, but has only one spouse at a time.

The labels can vary by textbook. The core definition to remember is “one spouse at a time.”

### B. Polygamy
**Polygamy** is marriage to more than one spouse at the same time.

| Form | Meaning | Memory clue |
|---|---|---|
| **Polygyny** | One man has more than one wife at the same time. | One husband → multiple wives |
| **Polyandry** | One woman has more than one husband at the same time. | One wife → multiple husbands |
| **Group marriage** | A marriage arrangement involving a group of people. | Group → group |

### C. Types of polyandry
- **Fraternal polyandry:** the husbands are brothers.
- **Non-fraternal polyandry:** the husbands are not brothers.

The lecture mentions the Toda community of the Nilgiri Hills and the Nair community in a historical discussion. Treat these as textbook/historical examples, not as claims about every member of those communities today.

### D. Types of polygyny
- **Sororal polygyny:** the wives are sisters.
- **Non-sororal polygyny:** the wives are not sisters.

The lecture mentions Gond and Naga communities as examples. Verify community examples against your prescribed text before using them as definitive facts.

**Common mistake:** Polygyny and polyandry are not interchangeable. Polygyny = multiple wives; polyandry = multiple husbands.

## 8. Rules of marriage: endogamy and exogamy

### A. Endogamy
**Endogamy** means marrying within a specified group.

Possible group boundaries:
- caste or sub-caste;
- tribe or community;
- religion;
- class, region or other social group.

**Why may it continue?** Community identity, custom, family preference, familiarity and social pressure may influence partner selection. These explanations do not justify coercion or discrimination.

### B. Exogamy
**Exogamy** means marrying outside a specified group.

Examples of rules:
- gotra exogamy;
- clan or lineage exogamy;
- restrictions based on close kinship.

Exogamy can widen social relationships and avoid unions prohibited by a community’s rules.

### C. Endogamy vs exogamy

| Endogamy | Exogamy |
|---|---|
| Marriage within a specified group | Marriage outside a specified group |
| Example: caste endogamy | Example: gotra exogamy in communities that follow it |

A person may face both rules at once—for example, a community may expect marriage within the caste but outside the person’s gotra.

### D. Gotra and pravara
- **Gotra:** a traditional lineage or clan identity in many Hindu communities, often linked by tradition to an ancient sage.
- **Pravara:** a traditional identification/invocation of ancestral sages in certain Hindu ritual traditions.
- **Gotra exogamy:** a custom in some communities that discourages or prohibits marriage within the same gotra.

These customs vary. They are not universal rules for every Indian community and do not replace the actual law governing prohibited relationships.

### E. Sapinda and prohibited relationships
The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 contains rules about **prohibited degrees of relationship** and **sapinda relationships**. Recognised customs may create exceptions in specified circumstances. Do not assume that every cousin marriage is prohibited throughout India; the rules depend on the applicable law and recognised custom.

## 9. Traditional Hindu forms of marriage

Classical Hindu texts describe **eight forms of marriage**. These are historical/religious classifications; do not assume all are equally accepted or practised today.

| Form | Basic meaning |
|---|---|
| **Brahma** | Giving a daughter in marriage to a suitable, learned man, without a bride-price. |
| **Daiva** | Giving a daughter to a priest associated with a sacrifice. |
| **Arsha** | Marriage associated with a symbolic gift, traditionally described as a cow and a bull. |
| **Prajapatya** | Marriage emphasising the couple’s joint performance of duties. |
| **Asura** | Marriage involving payment or wealth given to the bride’s family; often described as bride-price. |
| **Gandharva** | Union based on mutual love or choice. |
| **Rakshasa** | Marriage by forcible abduction, described in classical texts; not a lawful justification for coercion. |
| **Paishacha** | A condemned form involving exploitation of a person unable to give consent. |

**High-priority memory point:** Brahma, Daiva, Arsha, Prajapatya, Asura, Gandharva, Rakshasa, Paishacha.

**Important:** These are historical textual categories, not a statement that coercion or sexual exploitation is acceptable. Consent and current law matter.

## 10. Hindu marriage: law and ceremonies

### Hindu Marriage Act, 1955
This Act codifies important aspects of Hindu marriage, including conditions for marriage, ceremonies, registration, void and voidable marriages, judicial separation and divorce. Its statutory scope includes Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs as specified in Section 2, subject to the Act’s provisions and exceptions.

### Important conditions under Section 5
A marriage under the Act must meet statutory conditions, including:
1. Neither party has a spouse living at the time of marriage.
2. Each party meets the minimum age requirement.
3. The parties are not within prohibited degrees of relationship, unless a recognised custom permits it.
4. The parties are not sapindas of each other, unless a recognised custom permits it.
5. The parties satisfy the Act’s requirements concerning mental capacity and valid consent.

### Ceremonies and registration
- **Section 7:** ceremonies according to the customary rites and ceremonies of either party.
- **Section 8:** registration of Hindu marriages.
- Registration provides official evidence, but validity also depends on the applicable legal requirements.

### Void and voidable marriage
- **Void marriage:** legally invalid on a ground specified by law.
- **Voidable marriage:** remains effective unless annulled by a competent court on a legally recognised ground.

### Divorce and judicial separation
- **Divorce:** legally ends the marriage.
- **Judicial separation:** permits spouses to live separately under a court decree but does not itself dissolve the marriage.
- **Mutual-consent divorce:** available under statutory conditions, including Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act.

**Legal correction:** The lecture’s statement that every bigamy/desertion case automatically results in a fixed jail term is misleading. Penalties and legal consequences depend on the applicable provision and facts.

## 11. Muslim marriage and divorce: core terms

### Nikah
**Nikah** is commonly understood as a marriage contract under Muslim law, with rights and obligations for the parties.

### Terms to remember
- **Mahr/mehr:** financial entitlement owed to the wife under Muslim marriage law.
- **Nikahnama:** document recording marriage details and terms.
- **Talaq:** a form of divorce initiated by the husband under relevant rules of Muslim law.
- **Khula:** a form of dissolution sought by the wife under applicable legal principles and procedures.
- **Mubarat:** dissolution by mutual agreement.
- **Iddat:** a waiting period applicable in specified circumstances after divorce or the death of a husband.

Traditional classifications of marriage may use:
- **Sahih:** valid;
- **Fasid:** irregular, in classifications that recognise this category;
- **Batil:** void.

The consequences depend on the relevant legal school, statute and circumstances; avoid treating every category as identical in every context.

### Key statutes
- **Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937:** provides for application of Muslim personal law to specified matters.
- **Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939:** provides grounds on which a Muslim woman may seek dissolution of marriage.
- **Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019:** declares *talaq-e-biddat* (instant triple talaq) or a similar form of instant and irrevocable talaq void and illegal and provides statutory consequences.

**Correction:** Do not write that instant triple talaq is a legally valid divorce in India. The lecture’s claim that the 1939 Act was “updated in 1959” is not a reliable statement to memorise.

### Study points

## 13. Sikh marriage

### Anand Karaj
**Anand Karaj** is the Sikh marriage ceremony.

- **Anand Marriage Act, 1909:** recognises the validity of marriages duly solemnised according to the Sikh ceremony of Anand, commonly called Anand Karaj.
- The ceremony includes the **Lavan**, four hymns associated with the couple’s rounds around the Guru Granth Sahib.
- **Guru Granth Sahib:** Sikh scripture.
- **Milni:** a customary meeting of the families.
- **Langar:** community meal.

**Legal note:** The Anand Marriage (Amendment) Act, 2012 amended the law to provide for registration of Anand Karaj marriages. Registration and other matrimonial issues should be understood under the relevant laws. Do not memorise the lecture’s claims that every Sikh marriage must occur before sunrise or that every groom is given particular objects as universal legal requirements.

## 14. Parsi marriage

- Parsis follow Zoroastrianism.

## 15. Marriage-related social practices and reform

### A. Sati
**Sati** was a practice in which a widow was made or expected to die on her husband’s funeral pyre. It was prohibited by the Bengal Sati Regulation of **1829**, associated with reform efforts by **Raja Rammohan Roy** and action under Governor-General Lord William Bentinck. The practice is illegal and must not be romanticised.

### B. Widow remarriage
- **Hindu Widows’ Remarriage Act, 1856:** legally enabled Hindu widow remarriage.
- **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar** was a leading reformer associated with the campaign; Raja Rammohan Roy is primarily associated with the campaign against sati.

### C. Child marriage laws
- **Child Marriage Restraint Act, 1929:** commonly called the **Sarda/Sharada Act**.
- It came into force in **1930**.
- It was amended over time.
- **Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006:** the principal central law now addressing child marriage; it came into force on 1 November 2007.

**Current central-law age framework:** minimum marriage age is **21 for men and 18 for women**. A proposal to raise women’s minimum age to 21 in 2021 did not itself change the law. Do not write that women’s minimum age was changed to 21 in 2021.

### D. Dowry
**Dowry** is property or valuable security given or agreed to be given in connection with a marriage, as defined by the applicable law.

- **Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961:** prohibits giving and taking dowry and addresses related offences.
- A demand for dowry can have serious legal consequences.
- Do not confuse dowry with every voluntary gift; legal treatment depends on the facts and statutory requirements.

### E. Kanyasulkam
**Kanyasulkam** is a historical term for payment associated with a bride, discussed in Telugu social history and social reform. Do not automatically treat it as identical to the legal definition of dowry; explain its historical context.

### F. Domestic violence
- **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005:** provides civil-law protection and remedies for women facing domestic violence.
- It is not accurate to summarise it as a law that automatically results in arrest in every case. Remedies and procedure depend on the Act and circumstances.

## 17. Uniform Civil Code (UCC)

### Constitutional basis
**Article 44** of the Constitution is a **Directive Principle of State Policy (DPSP)**. It says that the State shall endeavour to secure a Uniform Civil Code for citizens throughout India.

### Meaning
A **Uniform Civil Code** is a proposed common civil-law framework for personal matters such as marriage, divorce, adoption, guardianship and succession, instead of different rules based on religious personal laws.

### Objectives commonly discussed
- Equality before the law;
- gender justice and reduction of discrimination;
- clarity and consistency in civil matters;
- reform of unequal personal-law rules;
- a common legal framework for citizens.

### Issues in the debate
- protecting religious freedom and cultural diversity;
- ensuring that any common law treats groups fairly;
- consulting affected communities;
- balancing equality with constitutional protections.

### Exam points
- Article 44 is in **Part IV** of the Constitution.
- DPSPs are **not directly enforceable by courts** in the same way as Fundamental Rights.
- Article 44 is a constitutional directive, not itself a complete UCC statute.
- Do not claim that a UCC has already replaced all personal laws across India.
- Goa is often discussed in UCC debates because of its civil-code framework, but do not oversimplify it as a perfectly identical code with no exceptions.

## A. Verified related previous-paper question

An official APPSC Combined Competitive Examination **Mains Sociology Paper-II (2015)** includes the short-note topic **“Problem of dowry.”** This is a related APPSC Sociology paper question, but it is **not verified as an APPSC Group 2 question**.

### Model answer: Problem of dowry
Dowry is property or valuable security given or agreed to be given in connection with marriage, within the meaning of the law. Dowry can create financial pressure on the bride’s family and contribute to harassment, unequal treatment and violence against women. The Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 prohibits giving and taking dowry. Effective prevention requires law enforcement, awareness, education, gender equality and social rejection of dowry demands.
