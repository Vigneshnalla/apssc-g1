# KINSHIP AND FAMILY IN INDIAN SOCIETY

**APPSC Group 2 — Indian Society | Consolidated Revision Notes**

## 1. Meaning of Kinship

**Kinship** refers to social relationships that connect people as relatives through biological descent, marriage, adoption, or socially recognised relationships.

**Examples:** Parents and children, siblings, spouses, grandparents, and relatives by marriage.

Kinship influences marriage, family responsibilities, inheritance, social identity, and relationships within a community.

### Types of Kinship

**1. Consanguineal kinship**

* Relationships based on biological descent.
* Example: A person and their biological brother or grandmother.

**2. Affinal kinship**

* Relationships established through marriage.
* Example: A woman and her mother-in-law.

**3. Fictive kinship**

* Relationships in which people treat one another as relatives without a biological or marital relationship.
* Example: Calling a close family friend “uncle.”

**4. Kinship through adoption**

* A family relationship legally established through adoption.
* Example: An adopted child becomes the legally recognised child of the adoptive parents.

**Remember:** Adoption creates a formal family relationship; it should not automatically be classified as fictive kinship.

## 2. Degrees of Kinship

Kin can be classified according to their relationship to a particular person.

* **Primary kin:** Directly related people, such as parents, spouse, and children.
* **Secondary kin:** Primary relatives of one's primary relatives, such as an uncle or aunt.
* **Tertiary kin:** Secondary relatives of one's primary relatives or relatives of secondary kin, depending on the classification used.

**Quick revision:** Primary = direct relatives; secondary = relatives one step further away; tertiary = more distant connections. Exact classifications may vary by textbook.

## 3. Functions and Importance of Kinship

Kinship helps organise relationships within families and communities.

1. **Social support:** Relatives help during illness, emergencies, marriages, and funerals.
2. **Social identity:** Kinship connects people to their family and ancestry.
3. **Marriage regulation:** Kinship rules may specify whom a person can or cannot marry.
4. **Property and inheritance:** Kinship may influence the transfer of property, subject to applicable law.
5. **Economic cooperation:** Relatives may share money, labour, business connections, and resources.
6. **Roles and responsibilities:** Kinship creates expectations regarding childcare, elder care, and family duties.
7. **Political relations:** Family networks may influence local leadership, alliances, and political support.
8. **Social organisation:** Kinship connects households and helps maintain community relationships.

**Rural India:** Kinship networks can be especially important for agricultural work, marriage arrangements, financial assistance, and mutual support.

## 4. Kinship Terminology

**Kinship terminology** means the words used to identify and describe relatives.

| Term               | Meaning                                     | Example                 |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Elementary terms   | Basic kinship words                         | Mother, father, brother |
| Derivative terms   | Terms derived from other relationship terms | Grandfather             |
| Descriptive terms  | Terms specifying the exact relationship     | Father's elder brother  |
| Terms of address   | Words used when speaking to a relative      | Calling someone “Uncle” |
| Terms of reference | Words used when talking about a relative    | “My father's brother”   |

Kinship terms may communicate not only relationships but also age, status, respect, and expected behaviour.

### Kinship Terminology Systems

Anthropologists compare the ways different societies classify relatives.

Important systems include:

* Eskimo
* Hawaiian
* Iroquois
* Crow
* Omaha
* Sudanese

**Remember:**

* **Lewis Henry Morgan:** Influential early comparative study of kinship systems and terminology.
* **G. P. Murdock:** Comparative classification of kinship terminology systems, including the systems listed above.

These are systems for classifying relatives, not types of family residence or descent.

## 5. Descent Systems

**Descent** refers to how a person traces family or group membership across generations.

### A. Patrilineal Descent

Descent is traced through the father's line.

**Example:** A family traces its lineage through the father, grandfather, and great-grandfather.

### B. Matrilineal Descent

Descent is traced through the mother's line.

**Example:** A child belongs to a lineage traced through the mother and maternal ancestors.

**Indian examples:** Khasi and Garo communities of Meghalaya; traditional Nair communities of Kerala.

### C. Bilateral Descent

Relatives on both the mother's and father's sides are recognised.

**Example:** A person maintains family relationships with both maternal and paternal relatives.

**Important distinction:**

* Descent = how family or group membership is traced.
* Inheritance = how property or assets are transferred.
* Residence = where a couple lives.
* Authority = who holds decision-making power.

These concepts are related but are not interchangeable.

## 6. Kinship Groups

### A. Kindred

An individual's network of relatives from both the mother's and father's sides.

**Example:** Your parents, siblings, maternal uncle, paternal aunt, and cousins.

A kindred is organised around a particular individual; therefore, two siblings may have overlapping but not identical networks of relatives.

### B. Lineage

A group whose members trace descent from a common ancestor through a recognised genealogical connection.

**Example:** Several generations tracing their descent from the same known grandfather through the father's line.

Types:

* **Patrilineage:** Descent through male ancestors.
* **Matrilineage:** Descent through female ancestors.

### C. Clan

A group whose members claim descent from a common ancestor, although the exact genealogical connection may not be traceable.

* The ancestor may be historical, mythical, or symbolic.
* A clan may contain several lineages.
* Clans are often exogamous, depending on the society's rules.

**Example:** Members of a traditional clan or gotra identify with a common ancestry and may be expected to marry outside that group.

### D. Phratry

A larger group consisting of two or more related clans.

### E. Moiety

One of the two major divisions of a society. The word *moiety* means “half.”

Some societies organise marriage and other social relationships around these two divisions.

### F. Tribe

A broader social group that may share community identity, cultural traditions, territory, and social institutions. A tribe is not simply another type of kinship group.

### Quick Comparison

| Group   | Key meaning                                             |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Kindred | One individual's relatives from both sides              |
| Lineage | Traceable descent from a common ancestor                |
| Clan    | Claimed common ancestry; exact links may be untraceable |
| Phratry | Group of two or more clans                              |
| Moiety  | One of two major social divisions                       |
| Tribe   | Broader community with shared identity and traditions   |

**Revision sequence:** A lineage may form part of a clan; several clans may form a phratry. A moiety divides a society into two major groups. These arrangements vary between societies and do not form a universal hierarchy.

## 7. Kinship Behaviour

Kinship behaviour refers to the customs and expected ways relatives interact with one another.

### A. Avoidance Relationship

Certain relatives are expected to maintain distance or restrict direct interaction.

**Example:** In some communities, a daughter-in-law may observe formal behaviour towards her father-in-law.

**Purpose:** Traditionally associated with respect, social boundaries, and preventing conflict.

### B. Joking Relationship

Certain relatives are permitted or expected to joke and tease one another playfully.

**Example:** Some societies permit playful teasing between particular in-laws.

**Difference:** Avoidance involves social distance; a joking relationship permits playful familiarity.

### C. Teknonymy

Referring to a person through their child rather than using their personal name.

**Example:** Calling someone “Ravi's father” instead of using their name.

### D. Avunculate

A culturally recognised special relationship between a person and their **maternal uncle (mother's brother)**.

In some societies, the maternal uncle has important responsibilities towards his sister's children.

### E. Amitate

A culturally recognised special relationship between a person and their **paternal aunt (father's sister)**.

The precise responsibilities and privileges vary across societies.

**Memory aid:**

* Avunculate = mother's brother.
* Amitate = father's sister.

### F. Couvade

A cultural practice in which a husband or father symbolically participates in aspects of pregnancy or childbirth, sometimes by observing restrictions or prescribed practices.

**Example:** A father observes particular food restrictions or rest practices around childbirth as part of a community custom.

## 8. Marriage Rules and Kinship

### A. Endogamy

Marriage within a specified group.

**Example:** Marriage within one's caste, where this is the customary rule.

### B. Exogamy

Marriage outside a specified group.

**Examples:** Clan exogamy or village exogamy.

**Important:** Exogamy does not always mean marrying outside one's caste. The rule depends on the group concerned.

### C. Hypergamy

A marriage pattern in which a person marries into a group considered higher in social status.

Traditional discussions of Indian marriage often describe a woman marrying into a higher-status group as hypergamy, although usage and customs vary.

### D. Monogamy

Marriage involving one spouse at a time.

### E. Polygamy

Marriage involving more than one spouse at the same time.

* **Polygyny:** One man has more than one wife at the same time.
* **Polyandry:** One woman has more than one husband at the same time.
* **Fraternal polyandry:** The husbands are brothers.

**Example:** Historical ethnographic accounts document fraternal polyandry among some Toda groups of the Nilgiri Hills.

**Remember:** Divorce followed by remarriage is not, by itself, polygamy.

## 9. Family: Meaning and Characteristics

**Family** is a basic social institution formed through socially recognised relationships such as marriage, descent, and adoption. It performs important functions such as child-rearing, socialisation, economic cooperation, and emotional support.

The English word *family* came through the Latin *familia*, a term associated with a household and its members or dependants.

### Characteristics of Family

1. **Universality:** Families exist in different forms across human societies.
2. **Social institution:** Family fulfils important social needs and follows social norms.
3. **Primary group:** Family members often have close, personal relationships.
4. **Emotional bonds:** Family may provide love, care, and belonging.
5. **Economic cooperation:** Members may share resources and responsibilities.
6. **Socialisation:** Children learn language, values, customs, and behaviour through family.
7. **Continuity:** Families help transmit culture, social identity, and sometimes property across generations.
8. **Variation:** Family structure, residence, inheritance, and authority differ across societies and change over time.

### We-feeling

*We-feeling* means a sense of belonging and shared identity.

**Example:** Family members may feel united because they share family traditions, responsibilities, and experiences.

## 10. Functions of the Family

G. P. Murdock is commonly associated with four basic functions of the family.

### Four Functions

1. **Sexual:** Family and marriage systems regulate socially recognised sexual relationships.
2. **Reproductive:** Family is an important institution through which societies organise reproduction and continuity between generations.
3. **Economic:** Family members share resources and help meet needs such as food, clothing, and shelter.
4. **Educational:** Children learn language, customs, values, and social behaviour through early socialisation.

**Memory aid:** Sexual – Reproductive – Economic – Educational.

Other important functions include emotional support, protection, social control, inheritance, and transmission of social status.

## 11. Types of Family

### A. Based on Marriage

| Type        | Meaning                                           |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Monogamous  | One spouse at a time                              |
| Polygynous  | One man with multiple wives at the same time      |
| Polyandrous | One woman with multiple husbands at the same time |

### B. Based on Residence After Marriage

| Type       | Meaning                                                                                   | Simple example                                                   |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Patrilocal | Couple lives with or near the husband's family                                            | Newly married couple lives near his parents                      |
| Matrilocal | Couple lives with or near the wife's family                                               | Couple lives near her parents                                    |
| Duolocal   | Spouses maintain separate residences associated with their respective family arrangements | Husband visits the wife's household in a traditional arrangement |
| Neolocal   | Couple establishes a new residence separate from both parental households                 | Couple rents a new home after marriage                           |

### C. Based on Descent

| Type        | Meaning                                  |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Patrilineal | Descent traced through the father's line |
| Matrilineal | Descent traced through the mother's line |
| Bilateral   | Relatives on both sides are recognised   |

### D. Based on Structure

**1. Nuclear family**

* Usually consists of parents and their dependent children.
* Example: Two parents and their child living in one household.

**2. Joint or extended family**

* Includes a wider group of relatives, often across generations.
* Members may share residence, property, resources, and responsibilities.
* Example: Parents, their married son, daughter-in-law, and grandchildren living together.

**3. Compound family**

* In some classifications, includes more than one conjugal unit within a wider family arrangement.
* The exact definition varies by textbook; follow the terminology in the prescribed APPSC material.

## 12. Theories of the Origin of the Family

These are historical theories attempting to explain the development of family and marriage. They should not all be treated as scientifically established explanations.

### A. Sexual Promiscuity / Sexual Communism Theory

* Proposed that early human relationships were less restricted by the exclusive marriage rules found in many later societies.
* The theory is historically controversial.
* The attribution in the lecture transcript is uncertain; verify the scholar's name in the prescribed textbook before memorising it.

### B. Patriarchal Theory

**Associated scholar:** Sir Henry Maine.

* Emphasises the authority of the father or senior male.
* Traditional patriarchal arrangements often give men greater authority in the household.
* Patrilineal descent and patriarchal authority are related in some societies but are not the same concept.

### C. Matriarchal Theory

**Associated in the lecture:** Robert Briffault.

* Historical theories emphasised the importance of maternal relationships and women in early family organisation.
* The claim that all societies passed through a universal matriarchal stage is not established.

**Distinguish:**

* Matrilineal = descent through the mother.
* Matrilocal = residence near the wife's family.
* Matriarchal = women hold predominant authority.

### D. Evolutionary Theory

**Associated scholar:** Lewis Henry Morgan.

* Morgan compared kinship systems and proposed that family and social organisation changed over time.
* His work is important in the history of anthropology, but his proposed evolutionary sequence is not accepted as a universal sequence followed by every society.

### E. Monogamy and Marriage

**Associated scholar:** Edward Westermarck.

* Westermarck studied marriage and family systems and discussed monogamy.
* Monogamy means marriage involving one spouse at a time.

**Exam caution:** The lecture mentions six theories, but the supplied material does not clearly establish all six. Do not invent a sixth theory or memorise uncertain attributions.

## 13. Kinship in North and South India

Irawati Karve was an important scholar of Indian kinship who examined regional variations in family organisation, kinship terminology, and marriage.

The following are **broad traditional patterns**, not universal rules.

| Feature            | North India — broad traditional pattern                     | South India — broad traditional pattern                          |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Descent            | Patrilineal descent is common in many communities           | Patrilineal descent is common; matrilineal traditions also exist |
| Clan/gotra         | Gotra exogamy is important in many Hindu communities        | Marriage rules vary by community                                 |
| Village marriage   | Village exogamy occurs in many traditional communities      | Practices vary; no single rule applies everywhere                |
| Cousin marriage    | Often restricted in many traditional communities            | Certain cross-cousin marriages may be permitted or preferred     |
| Marriage alliances | Marriage may connect different families and villages        | Marriage may strengthen existing kinship ties                    |
| Residence          | Patrilocal residence is common traditionally                | Residence varies by community                                    |
| Terminology        | Terms may distinguish family side, status, and relationship | Terms often distinguish parallel and cross cousins               |

### Parallel Cousins

Children of same-sex siblings.

* Father's brother's children.
* Mother's sister's children.

### Cross-Cousins

Children of opposite-sex siblings.

* Father's sister's children.
* Mother's brother's children.

Some South Indian communities traditionally permit or prefer certain cross-cousin marriages, including marriage with a maternal uncle's daughter or paternal aunt's daughter.

**Important:** These patterns differ among communities, and modern practices may differ from traditional ones.

### Similarities Between North and South Indian Kinship

Both regions have communities in which kinship influences:

* Marriage rules and family alliances.
* Descent and social identity.
* Inheritance and family responsibilities.
* Caste and community relationships.
* Exchange of gifts during marriages and other ceremonies.

Traditional ideas of social hierarchy and ritual purity have also influenced kinship and marriage in some communities. These ideas have sometimes supported exclusion and discrimination; they should be studied as social practices, not as measures of human worth.

## 14. Matrilineal Communities in India

**Matrilineal society:** A society in which descent and lineage membership are traced through the mother's line.

**Remember:** Matrilineal does not automatically mean matriarchal. Women may be central to descent and inheritance without holding all political or household authority.

### A. Garo — Meghalaya

* Garo society traditionally follows matrilineal descent.
* **Machong:** A matrilineal clan.
* **Nokna:** The female heiress in the traditional inheritance arrangement.
* **Nokrom:** The husband of the heiress in the traditional system.
* The youngest daughter may traditionally inherit family property, although arrangements vary.

### B. Khasi — Meghalaya

* Khasi society traditionally follows matrilineal descent.
* **Khadduh:** The youngest daughter, who traditionally has an important role in inheriting ancestral property.
* **Ultimogeniture:** Inheritance by the youngest child.

**Distinction:** Matrilineal describes descent through the mother; ultimogeniture describes inheritance by the youngest child.

### C. Nairs — Kerala

* **Marumakkathayam:** A traditional matrilineal system of descent and inheritance associated with Nairs and some other Kerala communities.
* **Taravad:** A traditional matrilineal family unit.
* **Karanavan:** The senior male manager in the traditional Nair taravad.

Historical Nair family arrangements sometimes included visiting-husband relationships, but practices varied between communities and periods. Modern law and social change have substantially altered traditional arrangements.

### D. Matrilineal Muslim Communities of Lakshadweep

* Some communities have historically followed matrilineal traditions.
* Family and inheritance customs vary by island and community.
* **Taravad** is a term associated with traditional matrilineal family organisation.
* Duolocal residence has been documented in some traditional arrangements, but it should not be assumed to apply to every community or household.

### E. Toda — Nilgiri Hills, Tamil Nadu

* The Todas are an Indigenous community associated with the Nilgiri Hills.
* Historical ethnographic accounts describe fraternal polyandry among some Toda groups.
* **Fraternal polyandry:** A woman has more than one husband, and the husbands are brothers.

Treat this as a historical ethnographic example, not as a claim about every Toda family today.

### F. Namboodiri Brahmins — Kerala

* Traditionally associated with Kerala.
* Historical family organisation was generally patrilineal and patriarchal.
* **Illam:** Traditional Namboodiri household or family unit.

**Correction to the lecture:** Karanavan is particularly associated with traditional Nair matrilineal family management, not as a general title for Namboodiri family heads.

## B. Conceptual Questions

**21. What is the difference between matrilineal and matriarchal?**

**Answer:** Matrilineal refers to descent through the mother's line; matriarchal refers to a system in which women hold predominant authority.

**22. What is the difference between patrilineal and patrilocal?**

**Answer:** Patrilineal concerns descent through the father's line; patrilocal concerns residence with or near the husband's family.

**23. What is the difference between a lineage and a clan?**

**Answer:** A lineage has a traceable genealogical connection to a common ancestor; a clan claims common ancestry, but the exact connection may not be traceable.

**24. What is the difference between parallel and cross-cousins?**

**Answer:** Parallel cousins are the children of same-sex siblings; cross-cousins are the children of opposite-sex siblings.

**Answer:** Monogamy involves one spouse at a time; polygamy involves more than one spouse at the same time.

**26. What is the difference between patrilocal and neolocal residence?**

**Answer:** Patrilocal means living with or near the husband's family; neolocal means establishing a new residence separate from both parental households.

**Answer:** No. Descent and authority are different concepts.

**28. What is the difference between endogamy and exogamy?**

**Answer:** Endogamy means marrying within a specified group; exogamy means marrying outside a specified group.

**29. What is the difference between a kindred and a lineage?**

**Answer:** A kindred is an individual's relatives from both sides; a lineage is a descent group tracing a genealogical connection to a common ancestor.

**30. What is the difference between descent and inheritance?**

**Answer:** Descent concerns how family or group membership is traced; inheritance concerns the transfer of property or assets.

Kinship is a system of social relationships that influences marriage, descent, inheritance, family responsibilities, identity, and community organisation. Family is a major social institution that performs reproductive, economic, educational, emotional, and socialisation functions. To prepare for APPSC Group 2, focus on precise definitions, differences between similar terms, sociologists and their contributions, Murdock's four functions, regional kinship patterns, matrilineal communities, and direct factual questions.
