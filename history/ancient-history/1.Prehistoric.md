## 1. Overview of Prehistoric Ages

Prehistoric India is commonly studied through the development of stone tools, food-gathering and food-producing practices, settlements, and metal use.

| Age                             | Approximate period*                                                                            | Main characteristics                                                                              | Important sites                                                     |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age)    | Earliest human occupation; textbook dates vary, often extending to c. 10,000 BCE               | Rough stone tools, hunting and gathering, mobile lifestyle                                        | Attirampakkam, Soan Valley, Bhimbetka, Belan Valley, Narmada Valley |
| Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age)   | c. 10,000–6,000 BCE in a broad textbook framework                                              | Microliths, hunting, fishing, gathering, some evidence of animal management and semi-settled life | Bagor, Adamgarh, Langhnaj, Mahadaha, Sarai Nahar Rai                |
| Neolithic (New Stone Age)       | Began at different times in different regions; early farming in northwest India by c. 7000 BCE | Agriculture, animal domestication, polished stone tools, village settlements                      | Mehrgarh, Burzahom, Chirand, Daojali Hading, Koldihwa, Mahagara     |
| Chalcolithic (Copper-Stone Age) | Broadly c. 3000–700 BCE for various regional cultures; dates vary                              | Copper used alongside stone, farming villages, painted pottery, regional cultures                 | Ahar, Gilund, Kayatha, Navdatoli, Inamgaon, Daimabad                |

*These are broad study ranges, not fixed dates for the entire Indian subcontinent. Different regions entered each phase at different times, and some cultures overlapped.



## 2. Palaeolithic Age (Old Stone Age)

### 2.1 Meaning and period

* Palaeo = old; lithic = stone.

* It is the earliest major phase of the Stone Age.

* It is associated with early human evolution, stone-tool manufacture, and a hunter-gatherer economy.

* Some broad textbook timelines begin around 2 million years ago, but the earliest archaeological evidence in India is not identical to that of the world as a whole.

### 2.2 Way of life and characteristics

1. Subsistence: People hunted animals and gathered wild fruits, nuts, roots, and other edible plants.

2. Shelter: They used caves, rock shelters, and temporary shelters made from locally available materials.

3. Social organisation: Small groups cooperated in activities such as hunting, gathering, and protection.

4. Nomadic lifestyle: Groups moved in search of food, water, and suitable living conditions.

5. Fire: The use of fire, developed over a long period, helped with warmth, protection, and food preparation.

6. Art and symbolism: Some later Palaeolithic communities produced artistic objects and cave or rock art; many famous paintings at Bhimbetka belong to later periods, so not all should be labelled Palaeolithic.

7. Adaptation: Tool types and subsistence strategies varied with local environments and available resources.

### 2.3 Phases of the Palaeolithic Age

| Phase               | Broad textbook chronology                                              | Main tools and features                                                                  | Important sites                                                           |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Lower Palaeolithic  | Roughly 2 million years ago to 100,000 years ago in some broad schemes | Handaxes, cleavers, choppers and other large cutting tools                               | Attirampakkam, Soan Valley, Pallavaram, Narmada Valley                    |
| Middle Palaeolithic | Roughly 100,000–40,000 years ago in a simplified scheme                | Flake tools, scrapers, points and smaller tools made by striking flakes from stone cores | Belan Valley, Narmada Valley, Bhimbetka, some sites in the Kurnool region |
| Upper Palaeolithic  | Roughly 40,000–10,000 years ago in a simplified scheme                 | Blades, burins and more specialised tools; bone tools at some sites                      | Kurnool caves, parts of central India and the Deccan                      |

Note: These phase dates overlap across different archaeological classifications. The tool categories are more useful for distinguishing the phases than treating the dates as exact boundaries.

### 2.4 Important Palaeolithic sites

* Pallavaram, Tamil Nadu: Robert Bruce Foote discovered a Palaeolithic stone tool here in 1863.

* Attirampakkam, Tamil Nadu: An important prehistoric site with evidence of early stone-tool traditions.

* Soan Valley: Located in present-day Pakistan; associated with Palaeolithic stone-tool discoveries.

* Narmada Valley: Important for prehistoric archaeology and evidence of early human presence.

* Belan Valley, Uttar Pradesh: Contains evidence associated with multiple prehistoric phases.

* Bhimbetka, Madhya Pradesh: Famous for rock shelters and paintings spanning different periods.

* Kurnool caves, Andhra Pradesh: Known for prehistoric occupation and evidence including bone tools.

Exam point: Bhimbetka is important for rock shelters and paintings, while Pallavaram is associated with Robert Bruce Foote’s 1863 stone-tool discovery.

## 3. Mesolithic Age (Middle Stone Age)

### 3.1 Meaning and period

* Meso = middle; lithic = stone.

* It followed the Palaeolithic and preceded the Neolithic in the conventional three-stage Stone Age classification.

* In many parts of India, it is associated with the transition to the warmer climatic conditions of the Holocene, which began about 11,700 years ago.

### 3.2 Main characteristics

1. Microliths: Small stone tools, often used as components of composite tools.

2. Composite tools: Microliths could be fitted into wooden or bone handles to make tools such as arrows and cutting implements.

3. Subsistence: Hunting, fishing, and gathering remained important; the degree of animal management and early food production varied by region.

4. Settlement: Some groups continued moving seasonally, while others repeatedly occupied or established camps near water and food sources.

5. Art: Rock paintings depict animals, hunting, dancing, and other activities.

6. Burials: Certain sites provide evidence of deliberate burials and grave goods, offering clues about social practices and beliefs.

### 3.3 Important Mesolithic sites

| Site               | Region         | Key features                                                                         |
| ------------------ | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Bagor              | Rajasthan      | Important Mesolithic site; evidence associated with animal use and domestication     |
| Adamgarh           | Madhya Pradesh | Microliths and evidence relevant to prehistoric subsistence and animal domestication |
| Langhnaj           | Gujarat        | Microliths and human burials                                                         |
| Mahadaha           | Uttar Pradesh  | Human burials and evidence of Mesolithic life                                        |
| Sarai Nahar Rai    | Uttar Pradesh  | Burials and evidence of hunter-gatherer communities                                  |
| Bhimbetka          | Madhya Pradesh | Rock shelters and paintings from multiple periods                                    |
| Birbhanpur         | West Bengal    | Mesolithic stone tools                                                               |
| Chotanagpur region | Eastern India  | Prehistoric sites and evidence of stone-tool traditions                              |

### 3.4 Art and burial practices

* Rock paintings often show animals, hunting scenes, dancing, and group activities.

* Burials at sites such as Mahadaha and Sarai Nahar Rai provide evidence of funerary practices.

* Grave goods, where present, can help archaeologists study the practices and beliefs of communities.

Exam point: The characteristic tool type of the Mesolithic Age is the microlith. Do not confuse it with the polished stone tools characteristic of the Neolithic.

## 4. Neolithic Age (New Stone Age)

### 4.1 Meaning and period

* Neo = new; lithic = stone.

* The Neolithic Age is associated with the development of agriculture, animal domestication, polished stone tools, and more permanent settlements.

* It began at different times across India. Mehrgarh in present-day Pakistan provides evidence of early farming and herding from around the 7th millennium BCE.

* A broad date such as 8000–2000 BCE is only a simplified textbook range; it does not apply uniformly to every region.

### 4.2 Main characteristics

1. Agriculture: People began cultivating crops. Wheat and barley were important in the northwest, while rice and other crops were important in some eastern and other regions.

2. Animal domestication: Cattle, sheep, goats, and other animals were kept for food and other uses, with regional variation.

3. Polished stone tools: Ground and polished axes and other tools, including celts, were used for tasks such as clearing vegetation and working wood.

4. Permanent settlements: Many communities established villages, supporting more settled ways of life.

5. Pottery: Pottery was used for cooking, storage, and other domestic purposes. Its appearance and production methods varied between sites.

6. Food processing: Grinding stones and related implements helped process grains and other foods.

7. Crafts and division of labour: Different tasks were undertaken within communities. However, a rigid division of work between men and women should not be assumed for every Neolithic society.

8. Burials: Some sites contain burials and grave goods, which provide evidence of funerary practices and social life.

### 4.3 Regional cultures and important sites

### 4.4 Key concepts

* Pit dwellings: Houses or living spaces made by excavating the ground; a notable feature of Burzahom.

* Ash mounds: Large accumulations of burnt material found at certain South Indian Neolithic sites, commonly associated with pastoral activities involving cattle.

* Polished stone tools: Stone tools whose surfaces were ground and smoothed, rather than merely chipped.

* Food production: Growing crops and raising animals instead of relying exclusively on wild food resources.

Exam point: Remember the site associations: Mehrgarh—early farming; Burzahom—pit dwellings and dog burials; Daojali Hading—corded pottery and polished tools; Utnur—ash-mound tradition.

## 5. Chalcolithic Age (Copper-Stone Age)

### 5.1 Meaning and period

* Chalco = copper; lithic = stone.

* The Chalcolithic Age is characterised by the use of copper tools alongside stone tools.

* It saw the development of regional farming cultures, settlements, pottery traditions, and metalworking.

* A broad range such as 3000–700 BCE can be useful for revision, but individual cultures had different chronologies.

* The Chalcolithic was not a single uniform culture, and it did not follow the same sequence everywhere.

### 5.2 Main characteristics

1. Agriculture: Farming was an important occupation. Crops varied by region and included wheat, barley, rice, millets, and pulses.

2. Animal husbandry: Cattle, sheep, goats, and other animals supported subsistence.

3. Metalworking: Copper was used to make tools and other objects, while stone tools continued to be important.

4. Pottery: Painted pottery was common in several Chalcolithic cultures. Black-and-red ware is associated with some cultures, including Ahar-Banas.

5. Settlements: Many communities lived in villages near rivers, cultivable land, or other useful resources.

6. Housing: Houses could be made from mud, thatch, reeds, or wattle-and-daub, depending on the region.

7. Trade and exchange: Communities exchanged goods and raw materials; the extent and organisation of trade varied.

8. Burials: Burial practices differed between cultures. Grave goods can provide evidence about social practices and beliefs.

9. Social differences: Differences between houses, burials, and artefacts may indicate differences in wealth or status, but these must be interpreted carefully.

### 5.3 Major Chalcolithic cultures

| Culture    | Region                                           | Important sites                     | Key features                                                                      |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ahar-Banas | Southeastern Rajasthan                           | Ahar, Gilund                        | Distinctive pottery traditions, copper use, and farming settlements               |
| Kayatha    | Madhya Pradesh, Chambal region                   | Kayatha                             | Regional pottery and copper artefacts; an important early Chalcolithic culture    |
| Malwa      | Central India, especially western Madhya Pradesh | Navdatoli, Maheshwar                | Farming settlements and distinctive pottery                                       |
| Jorwe      | Maharashtra                                      | Inamgaon, Jorwe, Daimabad, Chandoli | Important farming settlements, pottery, and varied settlement and burial evidence |
| Prabhas    | Gujarat                                          | Prabhas Patan, near Somnath         | Regional pottery and settlement traditions associated with the Chalcolithic       |

### 5.4 Important Chalcolithic sites

* Ahar, Rajasthan: An important site of the Ahar-Banas culture, associated with copper use and distinctive pottery.

* Gilund, Rajasthan: Another major settlement of the Ahar-Banas cultural tradition.

* Kayatha, Madhya Pradesh: Type-site of the Kayatha culture; associated with pottery and copper artefacts.

* Navdatoli, Madhya Pradesh: Important Malwa culture site, situated near the Narmada River.

* Inamgaon, Maharashtra: A major Jorwe culture settlement, important for studying farming, houses, and burial practices.

* Daimabad, Maharashtra: Known for notable bronze objects, including famous animal figures and a chariot-like object.

* Maheshwar, Madhya Pradesh: Associated with the Malwa cultural tradition.

Important correction: Inamgaon is an important Jorwe settlement, but it should not automatically be described as having the largest Chalcolithic settlement or as the site of every major Chalcolithic innovation. The size and importance of sites depend on the archaeological evidence being compared.

## 8. Corrections to keep in mind for the exam

1. Dates are approximate: Do not treat the dates of each Stone Age as identical across all regions of India.

2. Palaeolithic tool phases: Handaxes and cleavers are especially associated with Lower Palaeolithic traditions; flakes, scrapers, and points are important in Middle Palaeolithic traditions; blades and burins are characteristic of many Upper Palaeolithic assemblages.

3. Mesolithic domestication: Avoid saying that full-scale agriculture and domestication were universal throughout the Mesolithic. Evidence differs by site and period.

4. Neolithic agriculture: Mehrgarh is an important early farming site, but farming developed at different times in different regions.

5. Koldihwa and rice: It is often cited for early rice evidence, but do not memorise “the world's oldest rice cultivation” as an uncontested fact.

6. Burzahom: Its strongest identifying features are pit dwellings and distinctive burials, including dog burials.

7. Chalcolithic pottery: Black-and-red ware is associated with Ahar-Banas, but not all Chalcolithic cultures used the same pottery.

8. Daimabad: Remember its bronze objects; avoid mixing it up with Inamgaon’s importance as a Jorwe settlement.

## 9. Quick revision — one line per age

* Palaeolithic: Hunting and gathering; stone tools; Lower, Middle, and Upper phases.

* Mesolithic: Microliths; continued hunting, fishing, and gathering; burials and rock art at some sites.

* Neolithic: Agriculture, animal domestication, polished tools, and village life.

* Chalcolithic: Copper used alongside stone; regional farming cultures and distinctive pottery.

Study tip: Memorise the four-age comparison first, then learn the site associations. For APPSC MCQs, site–feature matching is a useful revision method; for Mains, explain the transition in tools, subsistence, and settlement without assuming that every region followed the same sequence.
