# INTER-STATE RELATIONS

“The successful functioning of the Indian federal system depends not only on the harmonious relations and close cooperation between the Centre and the states but also among the States themselves. Hence, the Constitution makes the following provisions to promote friendly relations and cooperation among the States.”

### The Constitution provides for:

1. “Adjudication of inter-state water disputes.”
2. “Coordination through inter-state councils.”
3. “Mutual recognition of public acts, records and judicial proceedings.”
4. “Freedom of inter-state trade, commerce and intercourse.”

In addition, the zonal councils have been established by the Parliament to promote inter-state cooperation and coordination.

# INTER-STATE WATER DISPUTES — ARTICLE 262

“Article 262 of the Constitution provides for the adjudication of inter-state water disputes.”

**Adjudication** → Formal settlement of a dispute.

### It makes two provisions:

1. Parliament can make a law to provide for the settlement of disputes or complaints relating to the use, sharing and control of water of an inter-state river or river valley.

2. Parliament may also provide that neither the Supreme Court nor any other court is to exercise jurisdiction in respect of any such dispute or complaint.

### Laws Enacted by Parliament

Using this provision, Parliament enacted two laws in 1956:

1. **River Boards Act, 1956**
2. **Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956**

### River Board Means

Since the river is shared by multiple States, decisions about its development, regulation and use may require coordination between those States.

A River Board is a body created to help with this coordination and management.

The River Boards Act provides for the establishment of river boards for the regulation and development of inter-state river and river valleys.

The Central Government establishes a River Board when the concerned State Governments request it. The Board's role is to give advice to those States.

### Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956

“The Inter-State River Water Disputes Act empowers the Central government to set up an ad hoc tribunal for the adjudication of a dispute between two or more states in relation to the waters of an inter-state river or river valley.”

The tribunal's decision is final and must be accepted and followed by the States involved in the dispute.

If an **inter-State water dispute** is referred **[formally sent]** to a tribunal under this Act, **neither the Supreme Court nor any other court has jurisdiction to hear or decide that dispute**.

So far, the Central government has set up several inter-state water dispute tribunals.

# INTER-STATE COUNCILS — ARTICLE 263

Article 263 provides for the establishment of an Inter-State Council to promote coordination among the States and between the Centre and the States.

The **President can establish an Inter-State Council** whenever he/she believes that creating such a council would **serve the public interest**.

The President can decide the **duties, organisation and procedure** of the Inter-State Council.

The President has the power to decide what duties the Inter-State Council will perform.

However, **Article 263 itself lists certain duties/functions that the President can give to the Council**.

**Public interest** → Benefit or welfare of the general public.

### They are:

**1) The Council can investigate disputes between States and advise them on how to resolve those disputes.**

The Council can investigate and discuss matters in which the States have a common interest, or in which the Centre and States have a common interest.

The Council can make recommendations on these common-interest matters, especially to improve coordination of government policies and actions relating to them.

The Inter-State Council can enquire into inter-State disputes and give advice, while the Supreme Court has the jurisdiction under Article 131 to decide legal disputes between governments.

**Article 263 → Inter-State Council → Enquire & Advise**

**Article 131 → Supreme Court → Decide legal disputes**

The Council can consider both legal and non-legal disputes, but it can only give advice. Unlike the Council, a court gives a legally binding decision.

### Councils Established Under Article 263

Using Article 263, the President established certain councils to make recommendations and improve coordination of policies and actions in their respective areas.

1. “Central Council of Health and Family Welfare.”
2. “Central Council of Local Government.”
3. “Four Regional Councils for Sales Tax for the Northern, Eastern, Western and Southern Zones.”

# ESTABLISHMENT OF INTER-STATE COUNCIL

The Sarkaria Commission (1983–88) strongly recommended establishing a permanent Inter-State Council under Article 263.

It recommended that the Inter-State Council should be given a different name so that it would be clearly distinguished from other councils/bodies that may also be established under Article 263.

The Commission recommended that the Council should perform the duties mentioned in Article 263(b) and 263(c).

* **263(b)** → investigate and discuss matters of common interest.
* **263(c)** → make recommendations for better coordination of policy and action.

Following the recommendations of the Sarkaria Commission, the Janata Dal Government led by V. P. Singh established the Inter-State Council in **1990**.

It consists of the following members:

### Members

**(i)** “Prime minister as the Chairman”

**(ii)** “Chief ministers of all the states”

**(iii)** “Chief ministers of union territories having legislative assemblies”

**(iv)** “Administrators of union territories not having legislative assemblies”

**(v)** “Governors of States under President's rule”

**(vi)** “Six Central cabinet ministers, including the home minister, to be nominated by the Prime Minister.”

Five Ministers who are either Cabinet-rank Ministers or Ministers of State with independent charge, nominated by the Prime Minister, are permanent invitees to the Council.

It aims to improve coordination between them by examining, discussing and carefully considering these issues.

---

## Duties of the Inter-State Council

### Duty 1

> “investigating and discussing such subjects in which the states or the centre have a common interest;”

### Duty 2

> “making recommendations upon any such subject for the better coordination of policy and action on it;”

### Duty 3

> “deliberating upon such other matters of general interest to the states as may be referred to it by the chairman.”

---

## Meetings of the Council

> “The Council may meet at least thrice in a year.”

Its meetings are held **in camera** and all questions are decided by **consensus [general agreement]**.

# PUBLIC ACTS, RECORDS AND JUDICIAL PROCEEDINGS

In Constitution, the legal authority of a State is limited to its own geographical territory.

Hence, it is possible that the acts and records of one State may not be recognised in another State.

To prevent this problem, the Constitution contains the Full Faith and Credit clause, which requires States to recognise certain official acts, records and judicial proceedings of other States and the Centre.

The Full Faith and Credit clause lays down the following:

### 1. Full Faith and Credit

“Full faith and credit is to be given throughout the territory of India to public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Centre and every State.”

* The expression **‘public acts’** includes both legislative and executive acts of the government.
* The expression **‘public record’** includes any official book, register or record made by a public servant in the discharge of his/her official duties.

### 2. Proof and Effect of Acts, Records and Proceedings

The general rule of recognising the acts, records and proceedings of one State in another State is controlled by Parliament’s power to decide how they must be proved and what legal effect they will have.

**Example:**

If a court judgment is issued in State A and needs to be recognised in State B:

* **Proof →** How State B verifies that the judgment is genuine.
* **Effect →** What legal effect that judgment will have in State B.

### 3. Civil Judgements and Orders

“Final judgements and orders of civil courts in any part of India are capable of execution anywhere within India (without the necessity of a fresh suit upon the judgement).”

“The rule applies only to civil judgements and not to criminal judgements.”

A State's courts are **not required to enforce the criminal laws of another State**.

**Example:** A person lives in State A, later moves to State B, and does something that is a crime under State A’s law but not a crime under State B’s law; the courts of State B are not required to punish him under State A’s criminal law.

# INTER-STATE TRADE AND COMMERCE

“Articles 301 to 307 in Part XIII of the Constitution deal with the trade, commerce and intercourse within the territory of India.”

Article 301 provides that trade, commerce and movement throughout India should be free. The purpose of Article 301 is to remove barriers between States and treat India as one economic unit, so that trade, commerce and movement can flow freely across the country.

The freedom under Article 301 is not limited to activities between States. It also applies to trade, commerce and movement within a State.

**Article 301 can be violated if restrictions are imposed at the State boundary or at any stage before or after goods cross the State boundary.**

Article 301 generally guarantees freedom from restrictions on trade, commerce and intercourse. However, restrictions specifically permitted by Articles 302–305 are allowed.

### 1. Restrictions by Parliament

“Parliament can impose restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse between the states or within a state in public interest.”

Normally, Parliament cannot favour one State over another or discriminate between States. However, an exception is allowed when there is a scarcity of goods in any part of India.

### 2. Restrictions by State Legislature

“The legislature of a state can impose reasonable restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse with that state or within that state in public interest.”

A Bill proposing such restrictions can be introduced in the State Legislature only after obtaining the President's prior approval.

The State Legislature also cannot favour one State over another or discriminate between States.

A State can tax goods coming from another State only if similar goods produced within that State are taxed in the same manner.

This provision prevents a State from imposing discriminatory taxes that unfairly treat goods from other States differently from similar goods produced within the State.

### 4. Government Monopolies and Authority Under Article 307

The Constitution gives freedom of trade under **Article 301**, but this freedom does not mean that private citizens must always be allowed to conduct every type of business.

The **Article 301 freedom does not prevent the Centre or States from creating government monopolies**.

The government can create a monopoly in a particular **trade, business, industry or service**.

### Authority Under Article 307

“Parliament can give the necessary powers and responsibilities” means:

* Parliament can appoint an authority under Article 307.
* After appointing that authority, Parliament can give powers and duties to that authority.
* That authority will then deal with matters relating to freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse and restrictions on it.

However, no such authority has been appointed by Parliament so far. **[In USA such authority is known as Commerce Commission]**
