# Amendment of the Constitution

Like other written Constitutions, the Indian Constitution also contains a procedure to change or amend its provisions according to changing conditions and needs.

However, the procedure for amending the Indian Constitution is **not as easy as in Britain and not as difficult as in the USA**. In other words, the Indian Constitution is **neither flexible nor rigid but a synthesis of both**.

**Article 368 in Part XX** of the Constitution deals with the **powers of Parliament to amend the Constitution and the procedure for amendment**.

It states that Parliament, using its **constituent power**, can amend the Constitution by **adding, changing or repealing** any provision, following the procedure laid down for this purpose.

However, Parliament **cannot amend those provisions which change or destroy the basic structure of the Constitution**.

The Supreme Court established this principle in the **Kesavananda Bharati case (1973)**.

## Procedure for Amendment of the Constitution

### 1. Introduction of the Bill

An amendment of the Constitution can be initiated only by the introduction of a Bill in either House of Parliament [Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha] and not in the State Legislatures.

**State Legislature generally consists of:**
- Governor
- Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha)
- Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad) — only in states that have one.

### 2. Introduction by a Minister or Private Member

The Bill can be introduced either by a **Minister or a private member** and does not require prior permission of the President.

### 3. Special Majority in Parliament

The Bill must be passed in each House by a **special majority**, that is:
- A **majority of the total membership of the House**, and
- A **majority of two-thirds of the members of the House present and voting**.

Each House must pass the Bill separately.

### 4. No Joint Sitting

In case of a disagreement between the two Houses, there is **no provision for holding a joint sitting** of the two Houses for the purpose of deliberation [detailed discussion] and passage of the Bill.

### 5. Ratification by States — Where Necessary

If the Bill seeks to amend the **federal provisions** of the Constitution, it must also be **ratified [formally approved] by the legislatures of at least half of the States** by a simple majority, that is, a majority of the members of the House present and voting.

### 6. President's Assent

After being duly passed by both the Houses of Parliament and ratified by the State Legislatures, where necessary, the Bill is presented to the **President for assent**.

The President **must give assent** to the Bill.

The President cannot refuse assent and cannot send the Constitutional Amendment Bill back to Parliament for reconsideration.

The **24th Constitutional Amendment Act of 1971** made it obligatory for the President to give assent to a Constitutional Amendment Bill.

### 7. Constitutional Amendment Act

Once the President gives assent, the Bill becomes a **Constitutional Amendment Act**, and the Constitution is changed according to that Act.

Article 368 provides for amendments either by a special majority of Parliament or by a special majority of Parliament plus ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures by a simple majority.
- Ratification → formal approval
Simple majority → majority of members present and voting
Special majority → majority of total membership + 2/3 of members present and voting

However, some provisions can be changed by a simple majority of Parliament, just like an ordinary law is passed.

Notably, these amendments are not deemed [ not considered ] to be amendments of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368.

## Three Methods of Constitutional Amendment

### (a) Amendment by Simple Majority of Parliament

Some provisions of the Constitution can be amended by a **simple majority in both Houses of Parliament**, without following the procedure under **Article 368**.

#### Provisions That Can Be Amended by Simple Majority

1. **Admission or establishment of new States.**

2. **Formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of existing States.**

3. **Abolition or creation of Legislative Councils in States.**

4. **Salaries, allowances and privileges mentioned in the Second Schedule.**

5. **Quorum in Parliament**

**Simplified:**
The number of members required to conduct the business of Parliament can be changed by simple majority.

**Complex word:**
- **Quorum** → minimum number of members required to conduct a meeting.

6. **Salaries and allowances of the members of Parliament.**

7. **Rules of procedure in Parliament.**

**Simplified:**
The rules for conducting the business of Parliament can be changed by simple majority.

8. **Privileges of Parliament, MPs and parliamentary committees.**

The privileges of Parliament, MPs and parliamentary committees can be changed by simple majority.

9. **Use of English language in Parliament.**

10. **Number of puisne judges in the Supreme Court.**

**Puisne judge** → a judge of the Supreme Court other than the Chief Justice of India.

11. **Conferment of more jurisdiction on the Supreme Court.**

12. **Certain provisions relating to the official language.**

13. **Citizenship acquisition and termination.**

14. **Elections to Parliament and State Legislatures.**

15. **Delimitation of constituencies.**

16. **Union Territories.**

17. **Fifth Schedule—administration of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes.**

18. **Sixth Schedule—administration of tribal areas.**

### (b) Amendment by Special Majority of Parliament

The majority of the provisions in the Constitution need to be amended by a **special majority of Parliament**, that is:
- A **majority of the total membership of each House**, and
- A **majority of two-thirds of the members of each House present and voting**.

#### Meaning of ‘Total Membership’

The expression **‘total membership’** means the total number of members comprising the House, irrespective of whether there are **vacancies or absentees**.

**Example:**

Suppose a House has **100 total members**.

Even if only **80 members are present**, the first condition is still based on **100**, not 80.

#### Special Majority at All Effective Stages

Although Article 368 mentions the special majority mainly for the final voting stage, the **Lok Sabha Rules require the special majority requirement to be followed at all effective stages of the Bill**.

#### Provisions Covered

This method applies to:
- **Fundamental Rights**
- **Directive Principles of State Policy**
- **Other provisions** that do not fall under the **simple-majority category** or the **special-majority-plus-State-ratification category**.

### (c) Amendment by Special Majority of Parliament + Ratification by Half of State Legislatures

Those provisions of the Constitution which are related to the **federal structure of the polity** can be amended by a **special majority of Parliament** and also with the **consent of half of the State Legislatures by a simple majority**.

The other States do not need to approve the Bill. Once **at least half of the State Legislatures** approve it, the required **State-ratification condition** is fulfilled.

There is **no fixed time limit** for the State Legislatures to give their approval.

#### Provisions Requiring State Ratification

This method applies to:

1. **The method of electing the President**.
2. **Extent [scope and limits] of the executive power of the Union and the States**.
3. **Certain provisions relating to the Supreme Court and High Courts**.
4. **Distribution of legislative powers between the Union and the States**.
5. **Provisions relating to the GST Council**.
6. **Changes to any of the three Lists in the Seventh Schedule**.
7. **Changes affecting the representation of States in Parliament**.
8. **Article 368 itself**, which deals with Parliament's power and procedure to amend the Constitution.

## Criticism of the Amendment Procedure

### 1. No Special Body for Amendment

There is **no separate special body**, such as the **Constitutional Convention in the USA**, created specifically to amend the Constitution.

The power to amend the Constitution is mainly given to **Parliament** and, in certain cases, also to **State Legislatures**.

### 2. States Cannot Initiate an Amendment

The power to **initiate an amendment** to the Constitution lies with **Parliament**.

Unlike in the USA, **State Legislatures cannot initiate any Bill or proposal for amending the Constitution**.

**Exception:** State Legislatures can pass a resolution requesting Parliament to **create or abolish a Legislative Council** in the State.

However, Parliament may **accept, reject or take no action** on such a resolution.

### 3. Parliament Has a Major Role

A major part of the Constitution can be amended by **Parliament alone**, either by a **simple majority or a special majority**.

Only in a few cases is the consent of State Legislatures required, and even then, the consent of **at least half of the States** is sufficient.

In contrast, in the USA, constitutional amendments require ratification by **three-fourths of the States**.

### 4. No Time Limit for State Ratification

The Constitution does not prescribe any **time limit** within which State Legislatures must ratify or reject an amendment submitted to them.

### 5. Withdrawal of State Approval

The Constitution is **silent on whether a State can withdraw its approval** after it has already ratified an amendment.

### 6. No Joint Sitting

There is **no provision for a joint sitting of both Houses of Parliament** if there is a deadlock over a Constitutional Amendment Bill.

In contrast, a provision for a joint sitting exists in the case of an **ordinary Bill**.

### 7. Similarity with Ordinary Law-Making

The process of passing a Constitutional Amendment Bill is largely similar to the **normal law-making process in Parliament**.

Except for the **special majority requirement**, Constitutional Amendment Bills are passed by Parliament in a manner broadly similar to ordinary Bills.

### 8. Limited Details

The Constitution provides only **limited details** about some aspects of the amendment procedure.

Because the procedure is not fully detailed, there is greater possibility of **disputes being taken to the courts for interpretation**.

---

## Overall Assessment

Despite these criticisms, the amendment procedure has proved to be **simple and workable** and has helped the Constitution adapt to changing needs and conditions.

The procedure is:
- **Not too flexible** → so that the ruling parties cannot change the Constitution according to their **whims**.
- **Not too rigid** → so that the Constitution can still be changed when circumstances require it.

### K.C. Wheare

K.C. Wheare said that the Indian amendment procedure:

> **“strikes a good balance between flexibility and rigidity.”**

The Constitution is intended to be **stable and permanent**, but it cannot be completely unchangeable.

---

## Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's View

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar believed that the Constitution should **not be treated as absolutely final or unchangeable**.

The Constitution should remain **amendable when necessary**, so that it can adapt to changing circumstances.

---

## K.C. Wheare — Variety of Amendment Procedures

K.C. Wheare appreciated the **different amendment procedures** provided in the Indian Constitution.

India has:

1. **Simple majority**
2. **Special majority**
3. **Special majority + ratification by at least half of the States**

This variety is considered a **wise feature** because different Constitutional provisions have different levels of importance and therefore do not need the same amendment procedure.

### ⭐ Final Understanding

**Indian Amendment Procedure → Neither too flexible nor too rigid**

**Simple provisions → Simple majority**

**Most provisions → Special majority**

**Federal provisions → Special majority + State ratification**

**Overall → Balance between flexibility and rigidity**
