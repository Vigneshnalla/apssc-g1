# Emergency Provisions

T The Emergency provisions are contained in Part XVIII of the Constitution, from Articles 352 to 360. These provisions enable the Central government to meet any abnormal situation effectively. The rationality [ reason ] behind the incorporation of these provisions in the Constitution is to safeguard the sovereignty, unity, integrity and security of the country, the democratic political system, and the Constitution.

During an Emergency, the Central government becomes all powerful and the During an Emergency, the Central Government becomes highly powerful, and the States come under the control of the Centre. It converts the federal structure into a unitary one without a formal amendment of the Constitution. This kind of transformation of the political system from federal during normal times to unitary during Emergency is a unique feature of the Indian Constitution.

 In this context, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar observed in the Constituent Assembly that¹:

'All federal systems including American are placed in a tight mould of federalism. No matter what the circumstances, it cannot change its form and shape. It can never be unitary. On the other hand, the Constitution of India can be both unitary as well as federal according to the requirements of time and circumstances. In normal times, it is framed to work as a federal system. But in times of Emergency, it is so designed as to make it work as though it was a unitary system.

The Constitution stipulates three types of emergencies:

1. An emergency due to war, external aggression or armed rebellion (Article 352). This is popularly known as 'National Emergency". However, the Constitution employs the expression 'proclamation of emergency' to denote an emergency of this type.

2. An Emergency due to the failure of the constitutional machinery in the states (Article 356). This is popularly known as 'President's Rule'. It is also known by two other names State Emergency' or 'constitutional Emergency'. However, the Constitution does not use the word 'emergency' for this situation.

3. Financial Emergency due to a threat to the financial stability or credit of India (Article 360).

## Grounds of Declaration

Under **Article 352**, the President can declare a **National Emergency** when the security of India or a part of it is threatened by **war, external aggression or armed rebellion**.

It may be noted that the President can declare a National Emergency even **before the actual occurrence** of war, external aggression or armed rebellion, if he/she is satisfied [convinced] that there is an **imminent danger**.

### Separate Proclamations

The President can issue **separate proclamations** based on war, external aggression, armed rebellion or their imminent danger, even if another Emergency proclamation is already in force.

* This provision was added by the **38th Amendment Act of 1975**.

### External and Internal Emergency

* When a National Emergency is declared on the ground of **war or external aggression**, it is known as an **External Emergency**.
* When it is declared on the ground of **armed rebellion**, it is known as an **Internal Emergency**.

### Territorial Extent

A proclamation of National Emergency may be applicable to:

* **The entire country**, or
* **Only a part of the country**.

The **42nd Amendment Act of 1976** enabled the President to limit the operation of a National Emergency to a **specified part of India**.

### Internal Disturbance → Armed Rebellion

Originally, the Constitution mentioned **“internal disturbance”** as the third ground for the proclamation of a National Emergency, but the expression was too vague.

Therefore, the **44th Amendment Act, 1978** replaced the words **“internal disturbance”** with **“armed rebellion”** as the ground for National Emergency.

**“Internal disturbance” is no longer a ground for National Emergency, but it was a valid ground when the Emergency was declared in 1975.**

### Written Recommendation of the Cabinet

The President can proclaim [officially declare] a National Emergency **only after receiving a written recommendation from the Union Cabinet**.

This means the Emergency cannot be declared based only on the **Prime Minister's advice**. There must be the **concurrence/agreement of the Cabinet**, expressed through its written recommendation.

In **1975**, the then Prime Minister, **Indira Gandhi**, advised the President to proclaim Emergency without first consulting the Cabinet. The Cabinet was informed of the proclamation after it was made, so the decision had effectively already been made.

The **44th Amendment Act, 1978** introduced this safeguard to prevent the **Prime Minister alone from deciding to proclaim a National Emergency**; a **written recommendation of the Cabinet is required**.

### Judicial Review

The **38th Amendment Act, 1975** made the proclamation of National Emergency **not open to judicial review**.

However, this provision was subsequently **deleted by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978**.

In the **Minerva Mills case (1980)**, the Supreme Court held that the proclamation of National Emergency can be **challenged in court** on the following grounds:

1. **Mala fide** — made in bad faith or for an improper purpose.

2. **Wholly extraneous and irrelevant facts** — the decision was based on facts that have no relevant connection with the constitutional grounds for Emergency.

3. **Absurd or perverse** — the decision is so unreasonable or irrational that it cannot be legally justified.

**Simple meaning:**
The government’s decision to proclaim a National Emergency **can be examined by the courts** if it was made in bad faith, based on completely irrelevant facts, or is legally unreasonable/irrational.

## Parliamentary Approval and Duration

> “The proclamation of Emergency must be approved by both the Houses of Parliament within **one month from the date of its issue**.”

Originally, the period allowed for approval by the Parliament was **two months**, but was reduced by the **44th Amendment Act of 1978**.

> “However, if the proclamation of emergency is issued at a time when the Lok Sabha has been dissolved or the dissolution of the Lok Sabha takes place during the period of one month without approving the proclamation, then the proclamation survives until **30 days from the first sitting of the Lok Sabha after its reconstitution**, provided the Rajya Sabha has in the meantime approved it.”

### Duration of Emergency

> “Once both Houses approve the Emergency, it continues for **six months**, and can be extended to an **indefinite period** with an approval of the Parliament for every six months.”

The requirement that Parliament must approve the continuation of Emergency **every six months** was introduced by the **44th Amendment Act, 1978**.

> “Before that, the emergency, once approved by the Parliament, could remain in operation as long as the Executive (Cabinet) desired.”

### Dissolution of Lok Sabha During Continuation

> “However, if the dissolution of the Lok Sabha takes place during the period of six months without approving the further continuance of Emergency, then the proclamation survives until **30 days from the first sitting of the Lok Sabha after its reconstitution**, provided the Rajya Sabha has in the meantime approved its continuation.”

### Special Majority

Every resolution by Parliament approving the Emergency or continuing the Emergency must be passed by a **special majority in each House**, that is:

**(a)** A majority of the **total membership of that House**, and

**(b)** A majority of **not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting**.

### Example

Suppose a House has **100 total members**.

**For condition (a):**
→ More than 50 members must vote in favour.

Suppose **75 members are present and voting**.

**For condition (b):**
→ At least **2/3 of 75 = 50** must vote in favour.

This **special majority provision** was introduced by the **44th Amendment Act of 1978**.

> “Previously, such resolution could be passed by a **simple majority of the Parliament**.”

### ⚡ Quick Note — 44th Amendment: Complete Change

The **44th Amendment Act, 1978** made major changes to the Emergency provisions, including:

* Approval period reduced from **2 months → 1 month**
* Continuation requires approval **every 6 months**
* **Special majority** required for approval/continuation
* **Cabinet's written recommendation** made mandatory
* **“Internal disturbance” → “armed rebellion”**
* **Judicial review** restored

### Revocation of Proclamation

A proclamation of emergency may be revoked by the President at any time by a subsequent proclamation. Such a proclamation does not require the parliamentary approval.

The President must revoke a proclamation of Emergency if the Lok Sabha passes a resolution disapproving its continuation.
This safeguard was introduced by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978.
Before the 44th Amendment, the President could revoke the proclamation on his/her own, and the Lok Sabha had no control over its revocation.

The 44th Amendment Act, 1978 also provided that if one-tenth of the total members of the Lok Sabha give a written notice:

to the Speaker, or
to the President if the Lok Sabha is not in session,

then a special sitting of the Lok Sabha must be held within 14 days to consider a resolution disapproving the continuation of the Emergency.

A resolution of disapproval is different from a resolution approving the continuation of a proclamation in the following two respects:

1. The first one is required to be passed by the Lok Sabha only, while the second one needs to be passed by the both Houses of Parliament.

2. The first one is to be adopted by a simple majority only, while the second one needs to be adopted by a special majority.

- Majority of the total membership, AND
- At least 2/3 of members present and voting.
	
	
	
## Effects of National Emergency

A proclamation of Emergency has **drastic and wide-ranging effects** on the political system. These consequences can be grouped into three categories:

1. **Effect on Centre-State relations**
2. **Effect on the life of the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies**
3. **Effect on Fundamental Rights**

### 1. Effect on Centre-State Relations

When a **National Emergency** is in operation, the normal relationship between the Centre and States changes significantly.

The effect on Centre-State relations can be studied under three areas:

* **Executive**
* **Legislative**
* **Financial**

#### (a) Executive

* During a **National Emergency**, the Centre can give directions to any State about **how the State should exercise its executive power**.

> “In normal times, the Centre can give executive directions to a State only on certain specified matters.”

Thus, the **State governments come under the complete control of the Centre**, though they are **not suspended**.

#### (b) Legislative

* During a **National Emergency**, Parliament becomes empowered to make laws on any subject mentioned in the **State List**, so the **State Legislature no longer has exclusive legislative power over those subjects**.

* Thus, the normal distribution of legislative powers between the **Centre and States is suspended**, though the **State Legislatures themselves are not suspended**.

* In brief, the Constitution functions in a **more unitary manner rather than a federal manner**.

* The laws made by Parliament on the **State subjects** during a National Emergency become **inoperative six months after the Emergency has ceased to operate**.

* Notably, while a proclamation of National Emergency is in operation, the **President can issue Ordinances on the State subjects also**, if Parliament is not in session.

> Further, the Parliament can confer powers and impose duties upon the Centre or its officers and authorities in respect of matters **outside the Union List**, in order to carry out the laws made by it under its **extended legislative powers during a National Emergency**.

* The **42nd Amendment Act, 1976** made it clear that these Emergency effects relating to **executive and legislative powers** need not be limited to the State where the Emergency is specifically operating.
* They can also **extend to other States**.

#### (c) Financial

* During a **National Emergency**, the President can change the normal constitutional arrangement for sharing revenues between the **Centre and States**.
* This means that the President can either **reduce or cancel the transfer of finances from the Centre to the States**.
* The change in revenue distribution continues until the **end of the financial year in which the Emergency ends**.
* Every such financial order issued by the President must be **placed before both Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha**.

---

### Effect on the Life of Lok Sabha and State Assembly

* During a **National Emergency**, Parliament can extend the **Lok Sabha's term by one year at a time**.
* This one-year extension can be repeated as long as the **Emergency continues**.
* Even if the Lok Sabha's term was extended during the Emergency, that extension **cannot continue for more than six months after the Emergency ends**.

#### Example

The **Fifth Lok Sabha**, whose normal term was from **1971 to 1976**, was extended twice, each time by one year, during the National Emergency.

* **1971–76** → Normal term
* **1976–77** → 1st extension
* **2nd extension was approved, but Lok Sabha was dissolved in 1977** → Actual end: **1977**

> “Similarly, the Parliament may extend the normal tenure of a state legislative assembly (five years) by one year each time (for any length of time) during a national emergency.”

* Such extensions can continue during the **Emergency**.
* But after the Emergency ends, the extension **cannot continue beyond six months**.

## Article 358 — Restrictions After the 44th Amendment

The **44th Amendment Act, 1978** limited the circumstances in which **Article 358** can operate.

### 1. Limited to War or External Aggression

The **six Fundamental Rights under Article 19** are affected only when a National Emergency is declared on the ground of **war or external aggression**.

They are **not affected when the Emergency is declared on the ground of armed rebellion**.

### 2. Protection Limited to Emergency-Related Laws

Only those laws that are **related to the Emergency** are protected from being challenged under Article 19.

Other laws that are **not related to the Emergency** do not receive this protection.

**Executive action** gets protection only when it is taken **under a law that itself is related to the Emergency**.

---

### Article 359 — Suspension of the Right to Judicial Enforcement

Article 359 does **not suspend the Fundamental Rights themselves**.

The Fundamental Rights **continue to exist**.

What Article 359 suspends is the **right to approach the courts for enforcement of the specified Fundamental Rights**.

### Simple Example

Suppose the President issues an Order under Article 359 suspending the right to seek enforcement of a particular Fundamental Right.

* The **Fundamental Right still exists**.
* But during the period of the Presidential Order, the person **cannot approach the court to enforce that specified right**.

Therefore:

**Right continues to exist → Right to approach court for its enforcement is suspended.**

---

### Article 359 — Which Rights Are Covered?

Article 359 does **not automatically suspend the enforcement of every Fundamental Right**.

Only the Fundamental Rights **specifically mentioned in the President's Order** have their enforcement suspended.

### Duration

The President's Order can suspend the right to seek enforcement:

* for the **whole period of the National Emergency**, or
* for a **shorter period specifically mentioned in the Order**.

## Differences Between Articles 358 and 359

The differences between **Articles 358 and 359** can be summarised as follows:

### 1. Scope of Fundamental Rights

**Article 358** is confined to **Fundamental Rights under Article 19** only, whereas **Article 359** extends to all those Fundamental Rights whose enforcement is suspended by the Presidential Order.

### 2. Automatic Suspension

**Article 358** automatically suspends the Fundamental Rights under Article 19 as soon as the Emergency is declared.

On the other hand, **Article 359** does not automatically suspend any Fundamental Right. It only empowers the President to suspend the **enforcement** of the specified Fundamental Rights.

### 3. Type of Emergency

**Article 358** operates only in the case of **External Emergency** (that is, when the Emergency is declared on the grounds of war or external aggression) and not in the case of **Internal Emergency** (i.e., when the Emergency is declared on the ground of armed rebellion).

**Article 359**, on the other hand, operates in the case of both **External Emergency as well as Internal Emergency**.

### 4. Duration

**Article 358** suspends Fundamental Rights under Article 19 for the **entire duration of the Emergency**, while **Article 359** suspends the enforcement of Fundamental Rights for a period specified by the President, which may either be the **entire duration of the Emergency or a shorter period**.

### 5. Territorial Extent

**Article 358** extends to the **entire country**, whereas **Article 359** may extend to the **entire country or a part of it**.

### 6. Articles 20 and 21

**Article 358** suspends Article 19 completely, while **Article 359** does not empower the suspension of the enforcement of **Articles 20 and 21**.

### 7. Powers Given to the State

**Article 358** enables the State to make any law or take any executive action inconsistent with the **Fundamental Rights under Article 19**.

**Article 359** enables the State to make any law or take any executive action inconsistent with those **Fundamental Rights whose enforcement is suspended by the Presidential Order**.

---

## Similarity Between Articles 358 and 359

Both Articles provide immunity from challenge to **only those laws which are related to the Emergency**, and not to other laws.

Also, the **executive action taken only under such an Emergency-related law** is protected by both.

### 1. First Proclamation — 1962

The first proclamation of National Emergency was issued in **October 1962** on account of **Chinese aggression in the NEFA (North-East Frontier Agency, now Arunachal Pradesh)**, and was in force till **January 1968**.

Hence, a fresh proclamation was not needed at the time of the **war against Pakistan in 1965**.

### 2. Second Proclamation — 1971

The second proclamation of National Emergency was made in **December 1971** in the wake of the **attack by Pakistan**.

Even when this Emergency was in operation, a **third proclamation of National Emergency** was made in **June 1975**.

Both the **second and third proclamations** were revoked in **March 1977**.

### 3. Grounds of the Three Proclamations

The first two proclamations (**1962 and 1971**) were made on the ground of **‘external aggression’**, while the third proclamation (**1975**) was made on the ground of **‘internal disturbance’**, that is, certain persons had been inciting the police and the armed forces against the discharge of their duties and their normal functioning.

### 4. Emergency of 1975

The Emergency declared in **1975 (internal emergency)** proved to be the most controversial.

There was widespread criticism of the **misuse of Emergency powers**.

In the elections held to the **Lok Sabha in 1977** after the Emergency, the **Congress Party led by Indira Gandhi lost** and the **Janata Party came to power**.

This government appointed the **Shah Commission** to investigate the circumstances that warranted the declaration of an Emergency in 1975.

The Commission did not justify the declaration of the Emergency.

Hence, the **44th Amendment Act, 1978** was enacted to introduce a number of safeguards against the misuse of Emergency provisions.

## Use of Article 356

Since the Constitution came into force in **1950**, President’s Rule has been imposed more than **125 times**. On several occasions, President’s Rule was imposed without sufficient constitutional justification, but instead because of **political or personal interests**. Hence, Article 356 has become one of the **most controversial and most criticised provisions** of the Constitution.

For the first time, President’s Rule was imposed in **Punjab in 1951**. By now, almost all the States have been brought under President’s Rule, once or twice or more.

### Use of Article 356 in 1977

When general elections were held to the Lok Sabha in **1977** after the Internal Emergency, the ruling Congress Party lost and the **Janata Party** came to power.

The **Morarji Desai government** imposed President’s Rule in **nine Congress-ruled States**, arguing that their State Assemblies no longer represented the wishes of the people after the 1977 Lok Sabha election.

#### Nine States

* Rajasthan
* Uttar Pradesh
* Madhya Pradesh
* Punjab
* Bihar
* Himachal Pradesh
* Orissa
* West Bengal
* Haryana

When the Congress Party returned to power in **1980**, it did the same in **nine States** on the same ground.

### Use of Article 356 in 1992

In **1992**, the Congress-led Central Government imposed President’s Rule in the BJP-ruled States of **Madhya Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh and Rajasthan**, alleging that these State governments had **not properly implemented the Centre’s ban on certain religious organisations**.

In a landmark judgement in the **S.R. Bommai case (1994)**, the Supreme Court upheld the validity of this proclamation on the ground that **secularism is a ‘basic feature’ of the Constitution**.

However, the Court did not uphold the validity of the imposition of President’s Rule in:

* **Nagaland — 1988**
* **Karnataka — 1989**
* **Meghalaya — 1991**

### Dr. B.R. Ambedkar’s View

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, while replying to the critics of this provision in the Constituent Assembly, hoped that the drastic power conferred by **Article 356** would remain a **‘dead-letter’** and would be used only as a **measure of last resort**.

Ambedkar expected Article 356 to remain a **rarely used constitutional safeguard**, rather than a routine instrument of Central intervention in State governments.

**“Dead-letter”** means a constitutional provision that exists but is **rarely or never actually used**.

Ambedkar also stated:

> “The intervention of the Centre must be deemed to be barred, because that would be an invasion on the sovereign authority of the province (state). That is a fundamental proposition which we must accept by reason of the fact that we have a Federal Constitution. That being so, if the Centre is to interfere in the administration of provincial affairs, it must be under some obligation which the Constitution imposes upon the Centre. The proper thing we ought to expect is that such Articles will never be called into operation and that they would remain a dead-letter. If at all they are brought into operation, I hope the President who is endowed with this power will take proper precautions before actually suspending the administration of the province.”

However, the subsequent events showed that what was hoped to be a **‘dead-letter’ of the Constitution** had turned into a **‘deadly weapon’** against a number of State governments and Legislative Assemblies.

In this context, **H.V. Kamath**, a member of the Constituent Assembly, commented:

Scope of Judicial Review

The 38th Amendment Act of 1975 made the satisfaction of the President in invoking Arti-cle 356 final and conclusive which could not be challenged in any court on any ground. But, this provision was subsequently deleted by the 44th Amendment Act of 1978 implying that the satisfaction of the President is not beyond judicial review.

In the Bommai case (1994), the following propositions have been laid down by the Supreme Court on imposition of President's Rule in a state under Article 356:

1. The presidential proclamation imposing President's Rule is subject to judicial review.

2. The satisfaction of the President must be based on relevant material. The action of the president can be struck down by the court if it is based on irrelevant or extraneous grounds or if it was found to be malafide or perverse.

3. Burden lies on the Centre to prove that relevant material exist to justify the imposition of the President's Rule.

4. The court cannot go into the correctness of the material or its adequacy but it can see whether it is relevant to the action.

5. If the court holds the presidential proc-lamation to be unconstitutional and invalid, it has power to restore the dismissed state government and revive the state legislative assembly if it was suspended or dissolved.

6. The state legislative assembly should be dissolved only after the Parliament has approved the presidential proclamation. Until such approval is given, the presi-dent can only suspend the assembly. In case the Parliament fails to approve the proclamation, the assembly would get reactivated.

7. Secularism is one of the 'basic features' of the Constitution. Hence, a state govern-ment pursuing anti-secular politics is liable to action under Article 356.

8. The question of the state government losing the confidence of the legislative assembly should be decided on the floor of the House and until that is done the ministry should not be unseated.

9. Where a new political party assumes power at the Centre, it will not have the authority to dismiss ministries formed by other parties in the states.

10. The power under Article 356 is an excep-tional power and should be used only occassionally to meet the requirements of special situations.
