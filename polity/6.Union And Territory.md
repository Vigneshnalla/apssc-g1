**Articles 1 to 4 under Part I deal with the Union and its territory.**

# Union and Territory of India

## Article 1 - Name of the Country

“Article 1 describes India, that is Bharat, as a Union of States rather than a ‘Federation of States’.”

**Article 1 deals with two things:**

1. **Name of the country**
2. **Type of polity**

There was no **unanimity** in the Constituent Assembly regarding the name of the country.

**Unanimity** = complete agreement among everyone.

Some members preferred the traditional name **Bharat**. Others preferred the name **India**. Therefore, the Constitution uses both names:

> **“India, that is Bharat”**

### 2) Type of Polity

The country is described as a **Union**, although its Constitution is **federal in structure**.

### Why "Union of States"?

According to **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, the expression **“Union of States”** was preferred to **“Federation of States”** for two reasons:

1. The Indian Federation is **not the result of an agreement among the States**, unlike the American Federation.
2. The States have **no right to secede from the Union**.

Therefore:

> **India is an indestructible Union of destructible States.**

### Meaning
- The **Union cannot be broken by the States**.
- The **boundaries, areas and names of States can be changed by Parliament**.

The country is an **integral whole** and is divided into different States mainly for **administrative purposes**.

## Territory of India

According to Article 1, the territory of India can be classified into three categories:

### 1. Territories of the States

### 2. Union Territories

### 3. Territories that may be acquired

Territories that may be acquired by the Government of India in the future.

### First Schedule

The Constitution has Schedules along with its Articles.

The First Schedule contains:
- Names of the States
- Names of the Union Territories
- Their territorial extent (the area covered by a State or Union Territory)

**At present:** 28 States + 8 Union Territories

The constitutional rules generally apply to all States in the same way.

However, special provisions under Part XXI applicable to the States of Maharashtra, Gujarat, Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Goa and Karnataka override the general provisions relating to the states as a class

**As a class → considered as a group.**

---

### Fifth And Sixth Schedules

The Fifth and Sixth Schedules contain separate provisions relating to the administration of:
- Scheduled Areas
- Tribal Areas

within certain States.

---

### States

States are members of India's federal system and share a distribution of powers with the Centre.

### Union Territories

The union territories and the acquired territories, on the other hand, are directly administered by the Central government (the central government)

## Acquisition of Foreign Territory

Being a sovereign state, India can acquire foreign territories according to the methods that are recognised and permitted by international law.”

### 1. Cession

Cession means one country or authority transfers territory to another country through an accepted agreement or arrangement.

### 2. Occupation

Occupation means taking possession of territory that was not under the sovereignty of a recognised State.

### 3. Conquest

Conquest means acquiring territory through military victory over another State or ruler.
(A country defeats another State in war and takes its territory.)

### 4. Subjugation

Subjugation means bringing a territory or people under control by force.

Since the commencement of the Constitution, India acquired several foreign territories such as:
- Dadra and Nagar Haveli
- Goa, Daman and Diu
- Puducherry
- Sikkim

## Article 2 - Admission or Establishment of New States

Article 2 gives Parliament two powers:

### 1. Power to Admit a State into India

Parliament can **admit a State that already exists outside the Union of India** into India.

### 2. Power to Establish a New State

Parliament can **establish a new State that was not previously a part of India**.

**Article 3 deals with changes in the existing states of the Union of India.**

Parliament can:

**(a)** Form a new State by:
- Separating territory from an existing State
- Uniting two or more States or parts of States
- Uniting territory with a part of a State

**(e)** Increase the area of a State.

**(f)** Diminish the area of a State.

**(g)** Alter the boundaries of a State.

**(h)** Alter the name of a State.

## Article 3

Article 3 lays down two conditions in this regard:

### Condition 1 — President's Recommendation

A bill contemplating the above changes can be introduced in the Parliament only with the prior recommendation of the President.

**Simple flow:**

**Proposal to change a State → President recommends → Bill can be introduced in Parliament**

---

### Condition 2 — State Legislature's Views

Before recommending the Bill, the President has to **refer the same Bill to the State Legislature concerned for expressing its views within a specified period**.

**State Legislature's consent is NOT required.**

The State Legislature can:
- Agree
- Disagree
- Suggest changes
- **Express its views because the proposed change directly affects the State.**

**But Parliament is not bound by its views.**

This means **Parliament does not have to accept or follow the opinion given by the State Legislature.**

After the State Legislature gives its views, the Bill comes back to Parliament.

Parliament may make amendments (changes) to the Bill.

---

### Union Territories

In the case of a Union Territory, there is no constitutional requirement to refer the Bill to the concerned legislature for its views.

The Parliament can itself take any action as it deems fit.

---

**“It is, thus, clear that the Constitution authorises the Parliament to form new states, alter the areas, boundaries or names of the existing states without their consent.”**

## Article 4

Article 4 specifically says that laws made under Articles 2 and 3 are not considered amendments of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368.
**This does not require a special majority in Parliament under Article 368.**

## Exchange of Territories with Bangladesh

The **100th Constitutional Amendment Act (2015)** was enacted [officially passed] to give effect to the acquiring of certain territories by India and transfer of certain other territories to Bangladesh under an agreement between the Governments of India and Bangladesh.

### Under this deal:
- India transferred **111 enclaves to Bangladesh**.
- Bangladesh transferred **51 enclaves to India**.

**Enclave** → a piece of territory belonging to one country but completely surrounded by another country.

In addition, the deal also involved:
- **Transfer of territories under adverse possession** [areas actually controlled by one country but legally claimed by the other].
- **Demarcation** [official marking] of a **6.1 km undemarcated border stretch**.

For these three purposes, the amendment modified the provisions relating to the territories of **four states — Assam, West Bengal, Meghalaya and Tripura — in the First Schedule of the Constitution.**

For the historical context, see [Background of the Amendment](#background-of-the-amendment).

### Dhar Commission and JVP Committee

There had been a demand from different regions, particularly South India, for reorganisation of states on linguistic basis.

Accordingly, in **June 1948**, the Government of India appointed the **Linguistic Provinces Commission** under the chairmanship of **S.K. Dhar** to examine the feasibility of this.

The commission submitted its report in **December, 1948**, and recommended the reorganisation of states on the basis of **administrative convenience rather than linguistic factor**.

People were unhappy with the Dhar Commission's recommendation and led to the appointment of another **Linguistic Provinces Committee** by the Congress in **December, 1948**.

It consisted of:
- **Jawaharlal Nehru**
- **Vallabhbhai Patel**
- **Pattabhi Sitaramayya** [JVP]

“It submitted its report in **April, 1949**, and formally rejected language as the basis for reorganisation of states.”

However, in October, 1953, the Government of India was forced to create the first linguistic state, known as Andhra, by separating the Telugu-speaking areas from the Madras state. “This became the first linguistic State.”

Potti Sriramulu went on a hunger strike demanding a separate State for Telugu-speaking people.

He continued the hunger strike for:

**56 days**

and died during the agitation.

Once Andhra State was created, people in other parts of India also began demanding:

> “If Andhra can be created based on language, why not our language-based State?”

So demands increased throughout India.

### Fazl Ali Commission

The creation of Andhra State intensified the demand for other regions for the creation of states on linguistic basis.

“This forced the Government of India to appoint (in December, 1953) a three-member **States Reorganisation Commission (SRC)** under the chairmanship of **Fazl Ali** to re-examine the whole question.”

“It submitted its report in September 1955 and broadly accepted language as the basis of reorganisation of states.”

Four factors of the Fazl Ali Commission ⭐

The Commission identified four major factors.

1. Unity → Language → Economy → Welfare

“The commission suggested the abolition of the four-fold classification of states and territories under the original Constitution and creation of 16 states and 3 centrally administered territories.”

“By the States Reorganisation Act (1956) and the 7th Constitutional Amendment Act (1956), the distinction between Part A and Part B states was done away with and Part C states were abolished.”
“The States Reorganisation Act (1956) established the new state of Kerala by merging the Travancore-Cochin State with the Malabar District of Madras state and Kasargode of South Canara (Dakshina Kannada).”

## Background of the Amendment

#### 1. India–East Pakistan Boundary

India and Bangladesh have a common land boundary of approximately **4,096.7 km**.

In **1947**, British India was divided and Pakistan was created. One part of Pakistan was **East Pakistan**.

The **India-East Pakistan land boundary** was determined according to the **Radcliffe Award of 1947**.

Some disputes arose from the Radcliffe Award.

These disputes were sought to be resolved through:
- **Bagge Award, 1950**
- **Nehru-Noon Agreement, 1958**

### 2. Berubari Union Issue

However, the issue relating to the division of **Berubari Union** was challenged before the Supreme Court.

There was a proposal to divide and transfer part of **Berubari Union in West Bengal** to Pakistan.

This became a constitutional issue because the question was whether India could transfer its territory to another country under the existing constitutional provisions.

The Supreme Court held that **Parliament's power to diminish the area of a State under Article 3 does not include the power to transfer Indian territory to a foreign country.**

Hence, **Indian territory can be ceded to a foreign state only by amending the Constitution under Article 368.**

To comply with the opinion rendered by the Supreme Court, the **Constitution (9th Amendment) Act, 1960** was passed by Parliament.

However, due to continuous litigation and other political developments at that time, the **9th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1960 could not be notified in respect of territories in former East Pakistan (presently Bangladesh).**

---

## 1974 India–Bangladesh Agreement

On **May 16, 1974**, the **Agreement between India and Bangladesh concerning the demarcation of the land boundary and related matters** was signed between the two countries.

The purpose was to find a solution to the complex nature of the border demarcation involved.

However, this Agreement was not ratified because it involved, among other things, **transfer of territory**, which required a **Constitutional Amendment**.

It was also necessary to identify the **precise area on the ground** that would be transferred.

---

### 2011 Protocol

Subsequently, the following issues were identified and resolved by signing a **Protocol on September 6, 2011**:

1. **Demarcation of the undemarcated boundary**
2. **Territories in adverse possession**
3. **Exchange of enclaves**

This Protocol forms an integral part of the **Land Boundary Agreement between India and Bangladesh, 1974**.

The Protocol was prepared with the **support and concurrence** [agreement/approval] of the concerned State Governments of:
- Assam
- Meghalaya
- Tripura
- West Bengal
