# PRIME MINISTER

Under India's **parliamentary system of government** provided by the Constitution, the **President is the nominal executive authority (de jure executive)** and the **Prime Minister is the real executive authority (de facto executive)**.

In other words, the **President is the Head of the State**, while the **Prime Minister is the Head of the Government**.

# APPOINTMENT OF THE PRIME MINISTER

The Constitution does not give any specific procedure for the **selection and appointment of the Prime Minister**. Article 75 says only that the **Prime Minister shall be appointed by the President**.

If one political party has a **clear majority in the Lok Sabha**, the President normally appoints the **leader of that majority party** as Prime Minister.

But, when no party has a clear majority in the Lok Sabha, the President may exercise his/her **personal discretion** in the selection and appointment of the Prime Minister.

When there is no clear majority, the President usually appoints the **leader of the largest party or coalition** and asks that person to **prove majority support in the Lok Sabha through a confidence vote**, normally within the specified period.

This discretion was exercised by the President for the first time in **1979**, when **Neelam Sanjiva Reddy**, the then President, appointed **Charan Singh** (the coalition leader) as the Prime Minister after the fall of the **Janata Party government** headed by **Morarji Desai**.

The President may also need to use discretion when the **sitting Prime Minister dies suddenly** and there is no clear person to immediately succeed him/her.

Suppose there is **no clear majority in the Lok Sabha** after an election. The President may appoint a person as Prime Minister **before** that person has proved his/her majority in the Lok Sabha.

After being appointed, the Prime Minister must **prove majority support in the Lok Sabha within a reasonable period**.

* **If the PM fails to prove majority** → he/she cannot continue as Prime Minister.

For example, **Charan Singh (1979), V.P. Singh (1989), Chandrasekhar (1990), P.V. Narasimha Rao (1991), A.B. Vajpayee (1996), Deve Gowda (1996), I.K. Gujral (1997)** and again **A.B. Vajpayee (1998)** were appointed as Prime Ministers in this way.

In **1996**, the Supreme Court held that a person who is **not currently an MP can become Prime Minister**, but must become a member of either the **Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha within six months**. Otherwise, he/she must cease to be Prime Minister.

There is **no constitutional requirement that the Prime Minister must be a Lok Sabha member**.

For example, three Prime Ministers—**Indira Gandhi (1966), Deve Gowda (1996) and Manmohan Singh (2004)**—were members of the **Rajya Sabha**.

In **Britain**, on the other hand, the Prime Minister should definitely be a member of the **Lower House (House of Commons)**.


# OATH, TERM AND SALARY

Before the Prime Minister enters upon his/her office, the **President administers to him/her the oaths of office and secrecy**.

### Oath of Office

In his/her oath of office, the Prime Minister swears:

1. To bear **true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India**.

2. To uphold the **sovereignty and integrity of India**.

3. To faithfully and conscientiously **discharge the duties of his/her office**.

4. To do right to all manner of people in accordance with the **Constitution and the law**, without **fear or favour, affection or ill will**.

### Oath of Secrecy

In his/her oath of secrecy, the Prime Minister promises **not to disclose confidential government matters** to anyone, directly or indirectly, except when disclosure is necessary for properly performing official duties.

### Term of the Prime Minister

The term of the Prime Minister is **not fixed**, and he/she holds office **during the pleasure of the President**.

However, this does not mean that the President can dismiss the Prime Minister at any time.

So long as the Prime Minister enjoys the **majority support in the Lok Sabha**, he/she cannot be dismissed by the President.

However, if he/she **loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha**, he/she must resign or the President can dismiss him/her.

### Salary and Allowances

The **salary and allowances of the Prime Minister** are determined by Parliament from time to time.

He/she gets the **salary and allowances payable to a Member of Parliament**.

Additionally, he/she gets a **sumptuary allowance, free accommodation, travelling allowance, medical facilities, etc.**

In **2001**, Parliament increased the **sumptuary allowance from ₹1,500 to ₹3,000 per month**.


# POWERS AND FUNCTIONS OF THE PRIME MINISTER

The powers and functions of the Prime Minister can be studied under the following heads:

## I. In Relation to the Council of Ministers

The Prime Minister enjoys the following powers as the **head of the Union Council of Ministers**:

1. The Prime Minister recommends people to the President for appointment as ministers. The President appoints ministers based on the **Prime Minister's recommendation only**.

2. He/she allocates and reshuffles various **portfolios (departments)** among the ministers.

3. He/she can ask a minister to resign or advise the President to dismiss him/her in case of **difference of opinion**.

4. He/she **leads the meetings of the Council of Ministers and influences its decisions**.

5. He/she guides, directs, controls, and coordinates the activities of all the ministers.

6. He/she can **cause the Council of Ministers to resign by resigning as Prime Minister**.

Since the Prime Minister **heads the Council of Ministers**, the other ministers cannot continue in office when the Prime Minister resigns or dies.

In other words, the **resignation or death of an incumbent Prime Minister automatically results in the dissolution of the Council of Ministers**, creating a temporary gap until a new government is formed.

However, if an **ordinary minister resigns or dies**, only that minister’s position becomes vacant. The **Prime Minister decides whether to fill that vacancy**.

---

## II. In Relation to the President

The Prime Minister enjoys the following powers in relation to the President:

### 1. Principal Channel of Communication

He/she is the **principal channel of communication between the President and the Council of Ministers**.

It is the duty of the Prime Minister:

**(a)** To communicate to the President all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to the **administration of the affairs of the Union** and proposals for legislation.

**(b)** The Prime Minister must provide the President with information about **Union administration and proposed legislation** whenever the President asks for it.

**(c)** If the President asks, the Prime Minister must place before the Council of Ministers a matter that was decided by an **individual minister** but has not yet been considered by the Council.

### 2. Advice on Appointments

The Prime Minister advises the President regarding the appointment of several important **constitutional officials**, like:

* Attorney General of India
* Comptroller and Auditor General of India
* Chairman and members of the UPSC
* Election Commissioners
* Chairman and members of the Finance Commission
* And so on.

## III. In Relation to Parliament

The Prime Minister is the **leader of the Lower House**. In this capacity, he/she enjoys the following powers:

1. He/she advises the President with regard to **summoning [calling] and proroguing [ending] the sessions of Parliament**.

2. He/she can recommend **dissolution of the Lok Sabha** to the President at any time.

3. The Prime Minister **announces and explains important government policies in Parliament**.

---

## IV. Other Powers & Functions

The Prime Minister has various other roles. These are:

1. He/she is the **chairman of the NITI Aayog** (which succeeded the Planning Commission), **National Integration Council, Inter-State Council, National Water Resources Council** and some other bodies.

2. He/she plays a **significant role in shaping the foreign policy of the country**.

3. He/she is the **chief spokesman of the Union Government**.

4. He/she is the **crisis manager-in-chief at the political level during emergencies**.

   👉 During an emergency such as a **war, natural disaster, major security threat, or other serious crisis**, the Prime Minister takes the **main political leadership role in deciding how the government should respond**.

5. As a **national leader**, the Prime Minister meets different groups of people and receives their **representations about their problems**.

6. He/she is the **leader of the party in power**.

7. He/she is the **political head of the services**.

Thus, the Prime Minister plays a **very significant and highly crucial role in the politico-administrative system of the country**.

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar stated:

> **“If any Indian constitutional functionary is to be compared with the US President, it would be the Indian Prime Minister, not the Indian President.”**


# RELATIONSHIP WITH THE PRESIDENT

The following provisions of the Constitution deal with the relationship between the **President and the Prime Minister**:

## 1. Article 74

There must be a **Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister** to aid and advise the President.

The President must generally act according to the **advice of the Council of Ministers**.

However, the President may require the Council of Ministers to **reconsider such advice**, and the President shall act in accordance with the advice tendered after such reconsideration.

## 2. Article 75

**(a)** The Prime Minister shall be appointed by the President, and the other ministers shall be appointed by the President **on the advice of the Prime Minister**.

**(b)** The ministers shall hold office **during the pleasure of the President**.

**(c)** The Council of Ministers shall be **collectively responsible to the House of the People (Lok Sabha)**.

## 3. Article 78

It shall be the duty of the Prime Minister:

**(a)** To communicate to the President all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to the **administration of the affairs of the Union** and proposals for legislation.

**(b)** To furnish such information relating to the **administration of the affairs of the Union** and proposals for legislation as the President may call for.

**(c)** If the President so requires, to submit for the **consideration of the Council of Ministers** any matter on which a decision has been taken by a minister but which has not been considered by the Council.

Morarji Desai, Chief Minister of the erstwhile **Bombay State** during **1952–1956**, became the **first non-Congress Prime Minister** in March 1977.

He was succeeded as Prime Minister by **Charan Singh**, who had been the Chief Minister of **undivided Uttar Pradesh** in **1967–1968** and again in **1970**.

### 2. Charan Singh

Charan Singh was the Chief Minister of **undivided Uttar Pradesh** in **1967–1968** and again in **1970**. He later succeeded Morarji Desai as Prime Minister in **1979**.

### 3. V.P. Singh

V.P. Singh, who was also from **Uttar Pradesh**, became Prime Minister in **December 1989** as the head of the **National Front government**. This government lasted until **November 1990**.

### 4. P.V. Narasimha Rao

P.V. Narasimha Rao was the **Chief Minister of Andhra Pradesh from 1971–1973** and later became Prime Minister, serving from **1991–1996**.

### 5. H.D. Deve Gowda

H.D. Deve Gowda was the **Chief Minister of Karnataka** when he was chosen to lead the **United Front government** in **June 1996**.

### 6. Narendra Modi

Narendra Modi (BJP) was the **Chief Minister of Gujarat** when he became the **Prime Minister in May 2014**.

He served as the **Chief Minister of Gujarat for four times during 2001–2014**.

# CARETAKER GOVERNMENT

The **Constitution of India does not contain any provision for a caretaker government**. It is an arrangement that is used when a particular political situation requires it.

The following points clearly bring out the meaning of a caretaker government:

### 1. Formation After Dissolution of Lok Sabha

The formation of a **caretaker government after the dissolution of the Lok Sabha** and its replacement by a **new ministry after the general election** is an essential feature of the **parliamentary system of government**.

Such a government is expected to ensure that **free and fair elections are conducted**, so that a new elected government can be formed.

### 2. Continuation After Losing Confidence

The term **“caretaker government”** refers to a Council of Ministers that has **resigned after losing the confidence of the Lok Sabha** but is asked by the **President to continue temporarily until a new government is formed**.

If an alternative government cannot be formed immediately and general elections have to be held, the outgoing **Council of Ministers may continue until the elections are completed and a new government is formed**.

## Limited Role

Unlike a regular government, a caretaker government is meant only for **managing the routine activities of the government**.

Hence, a caretaker government should generally **not make major policy decisions or introduce new schemes**, except when necessary for **national security or national interest**.

The following comments and observations highlight the limited role of a caretaker government in the governance of the country:

### 1. Tarkunde Committee (1974–75)

The Tarkunde Committee recommended that a caretaker government should not:

1. Initiate and announce new policies.
2. Promise or start new projects.
3. Grant allowances or loans, or announce salary increases.
4. Hold official functions attended by ministers.

### 2. President N. Sanjeeva Reddy — August 1979

In August 1979, the then President **N. Sanjeeva Reddy**, while dissolving the Lok Sabha, issued a communique stating that the President had consultations with the Prime Minister and some of his Cabinet colleagues, who assured that:

**(i)** Elections would be **peaceful, free and fair**. The revision of the electoral rolls would begin immediately, and the election timetable would commence in November 1979 and be completed by December 1979.

**(ii)** The Government would not take decisions during this period that:

* Set new policies;
* Involve new spending of a significant order; or
* Constitute major administrative or executive decisions.

However, **urgent work involving the national interest would not be held up**.

### 3. Calcutta High Court — December 1979

The Calcutta High Court, in a judgment delivered in December 1979, observed that there is **no mention of a caretaker government as such in the Constitution**.

However, such an extraordinary situation calls for a caretaker government. Therefore, in such a situation, the **Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers can carry on only the day-to-day administration necessary until an alternative arrangement is made**.

### 4. Constraints on Caretaker Prime Ministers

Caretaker Prime Ministers are constrained for two reasons:

1. The **normal channel of accountability through Parliament does not exist**.
2. The government should **not abuse its position to gain electoral advantage**.

# ROLE DESCRIPTIONS

The Prime Minister is extremely important in both the **British and Indian parliamentary systems**. Therefore, the descriptions given by these scholars about the British Prime Minister can also be used to understand the position of the Indian Prime Minister.

These are mentioned below:

### 1. Lord Morely

He described the Prime Minister as **“primus inter pares”** (first among equals) and **“key stone of the cabinet arch.”**

He said:

> “The head of the cabinet is ‘primus inter pares’, and occupied a position which so long as it lasts, is one of exceptional and peculiar authority.”

### 2. Herbert Marrison

> “As the head of the Government, he (Prime Minister) is ‘primus inter pares’. But describing him only as ‘first among equals’ underestimates his actual position and power.”

The Prime Minister is the **first among equals** as the head of the Government, but describing him only as “first among equals” underestimates his actual position and power.

### 3. Sir Williarp Vernor Harcourt

He described the Prime Minister as **“inter stellas luna minores”** (a moon among lesser stars).

### 4. Jennings

> “He is, rather, a sun around which planets revolve. He is the key-stone of the constitution. All roads in the constitution lead to the Prime Minister.”

### 5. H.J. Laski

On the relationship between the Prime Minister and the Cabinet, he said that the Prime Minister:

> “is central to its formation, central to its life, and central to its death.”

He described him as:

> “the pivot around which the entire governmental machinery revolves.”

### 6. H.R.G. Greaves

> “The Government is the master of the country and he (Prime Minister) is the master of the Government.”

### 7. Munro

He called the Prime Minister:

> “the captain of the ship of the state.”

### 8. Ramsay Muir

He described the Prime Minister as:

> “the steersman of steering wheel of the ship of the state.”

## Prime Ministerial Government

The role of the Prime Minister in the **British parliamentary government** is so significant and crucial that observers like to call it a **“Prime Ministerial government.”**

Thus, R.H. Crossman says:

> “The post-war epoch has been the final transformation of cabinet government into Prime Ministerial government.”

Similarly, Humphrey Berkely points out:

> “Parliament is not, in practice, sovereign. The parliamentary democracy has now collapsed at Westminster. The basic defect in the British system of governing is the super-ministerial powers of the Prime Minister.”
