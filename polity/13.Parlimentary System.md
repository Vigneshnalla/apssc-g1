# Parliamentary and Presidential Systems

The Indian Constitution establishes a **parliamentary system of government** at both the **Centre and the States**.

* **Articles 74 and 75** → Parliamentary system at the Centre
* **Articles 163 and 164** → Parliamentary system in the States

Modern democratic governments are mainly classified into **parliamentary and presidential systems** based on the relationship between the **Executive and the Legislature**.

---

## Parliamentary System

A **parliamentary system** is one in which the **Executive is responsible to the Legislature for its policies and actions**.

The parliamentary system is also known as:

* **Cabinet Government**
* **Responsible Government**
* **Westminster Model**

It is followed in countries such as **Britain, Japan, Canada and India**.

It is called **Cabinet Government** because the **Cabinet is the centre of power in the parliamentary system**. Ivor Jennings described it as the **“cabinet system.”**

It is called **Responsible Government** because the **Cabinet is accountable to Parliament and can remain in office only as long as it has the confidence of the Legislature**.

It is called the **Westminster Model** because the system developed in **Britain**, with Westminster in London being the centre of the British parliamentary system.

---

## Presidential System

A **presidential system** is one in which the **Executive is not responsible to the Legislature for its policies and actions, and its term of office is constitutionally fixed and independent of the Legislature**.

It is also known as:

* **Non-responsible Government**
* **Non-parliamentary Government**
* **Fixed Executive System**

It is followed in countries such as **USA, Brazil, Russia and Sri Lanka**.

---

## Prime Ministerial Government

Traditionally, British constitutional writers described the **Prime Minister as “first among equals”** in the Cabinet.

However, in the modern period, the **power and influence of the Prime Minister have increased significantly** compared with other Cabinet ministers.

Therefore, political analysts such as **Crossman and Mackintosh** have described the modern British system as **“Prime Ministerial Government.”**

---

### ⭐ Core Difference

> **Parliamentary System → Executive is responsible to the Legislature and depends on its confidence.**

> **Presidential System → Executive is constitutionally independent of the Legislature regarding its term of office.**

---

# Features of Parliamentary Government

### 1) Nominal and Real Executive

The President is the **nominal executive** (**de jure executive or titular executive**) while the Prime Minister is the **real executive** (**de facto executive**).

The President is the **Head of the State**, while the Prime Minister is the **Head of the Government**.

Article 74 establishes a Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister to help and advise the President in performing constitutional functions.

> **The President is constitutionally required to act according to the advice of the Council of Ministers.**

---

### 2) Majority Party Rule

The political party that **secures a majority of seats in the Lok Sabha** forms the government.

The **President appoints the leader of the majority party as the Prime Minister** and appoints other ministers on the **Prime Minister's advice**.

If no single party gets a majority, **two or more parties may join together to form a coalition government** with the required majority support in the Lok Sabha.

> **Majority party → forms government → leader becomes Prime Minister → other ministers are appointed on the PM's advice.**

---

### 3) Collective Responsibility

This is the **bedrock principle of parliamentary government**.

Under Article 75, the Council of Ministers is **collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha**.

This means that the ministers work as a team, take collective responsibility for government decisions, and must have the confidence of the Lok Sabha.

The principle of collective responsibility implies that the Lok Sabha can remove the ministry from office by passing a **vote of no confidence**.

**No confidence** = “We do not support this government anymore.”

If the Lok Sabha passes a no-confidence motion, it means:

> “We no longer have confidence in this government.”

If the motion is passed, the **Council of Ministers must resign**.

---

### 4) Political Homogeneity

Usually, members of the Council of Ministers belong to the **same political party**, and hence they share the same **political ideology**.

In a coalition government, ministers from different parties generally work according to a **common agreement**.

---

### 5) Double Membership

Ministers are generally members of **Parliament** and are part of both the **Legislature and Executive**.

However, a person who is **not a member of Parliament** can also be appointed as a minister. Such a person must become a member of either House of Parliament within **six months**; otherwise, they cease to be a minister.

⭐ **Key Point:** A non-MP can become a minister, but must become an MP within **six months**.

---

### 6) Leadership of Prime Minister

The Prime Minister plays the **leadership role** in this system of government.

The Prime Minister leads the **Council of Ministers**, is the **leader of the government in Parliament**, and is generally the **leader of the party in power**.

Because of these roles, the Prime Minister plays a **very important part in running the government**.

---

### 7) Dissolution of the Lower House

The **Lok Sabha**, the Lower House of Parliament, normally has a term of **five years**, but it can be dissolved earlier.

The **President formally dissolves the Lok Sabha**, generally on the **advice of the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers**.

After dissolution, **fresh Lok Sabha elections are held** to form a new House.

---

### 8) Secrecy

Ministers follow the principle of **secrecy** and are not supposed to disclose confidential information about government discussions, policies and decisions.

Before entering office, ministers take an **oath of secrecy**.

The **President administers the oath of secrecy** to Union ministers.

⭐ **Key Point:** Ministers must maintain the **confidentiality of government and Cabinet discussions**.

---

# Features of Presidential Government

### (a) President is Head of State and Head of Government

In the **American presidential system**, the President is both the **Head of State and Head of Government**.

As **Head of State**, the President represents the country.

As **Head of Government**, the President is the **real executive** and exercises executive authority.

⭐ **Key Point:** **USA → President = Head of State + Head of Government**

---

### (b) Fixed Tenure

The President of the United States is elected through an **Electoral College** for a **fixed term of four years**.

⭐ **Key Point:** The President has a fixed four-year term and does not depend on the continuing confidence of the Legislature to remain in office.

The President cannot normally be removed by Congress and can be removed through the **constitutional impeachment process for serious wrongdoing**.

---

### (c) Cabinet / Kitchen Cabinet

The President governs with the help of a **Cabinet**, which is an advisory body consisting of **departmental secretaries who are not members of Congress**.

These secretaries are **appointed by the President**, are responsible to the President, and can generally be removed by the President.

The President may also rely on a smaller, informal group of trusted advisers called the **Kitchen Cabinet**.

⭐ **Key Point:** The President's Cabinet advises the President, while the secretaries are appointed and can generally be removed by the President.

---

### (d) Executive is not responsible to Congress

The **President and secretaries are not responsible to Congress** for their actions in the parliamentary sense.

They are **not members of Congress** and do not normally attend its sessions as members.

⭐ **Key Point:** The Executive and Legislature are separate, and the President does not depend on the continuing confidence of Congress.

---

### (e) President cannot dissolve the House of Representatives

The American President **cannot dissolve the House of Representatives**, the lower house of Congress.

⭐ **Key Point:** The President has no power to dissolve the House of Representatives.

---

### (f) Separation of Powers

The **separation of powers** is a fundamental principle of the American presidential system.

Government powers are divided among three organs:

* **Legislature** → makes laws
* **Executive** → implements and administers laws
* **Judiciary** → interprets laws and settles disputes

⭐ **Key Point:** Separation of powers prevents the concentration of government power in a single organ.

---

# Merits of the Parliamentary System

### 1. Harmony Between Legislature and Executive

The parliamentary system ensures a **harmonious relationship and cooperation between the Legislature and Executive**.

The **Executive is a part of the Legislature**, and both are **interdependent** in performing their functions.

Since they work closely together, there is **less possibility of conflict between the Legislature and Executive**.

---

### 2. Responsible Government

The parliamentary system establishes a **responsible government**, where the Council of Ministers is responsible to the **Lok Sabha**.

Ministers are responsible to Parliament for all their acts of **omission and commission**.

* **Omission** → failure to do something
* **Commission** → an act or something that is done

Parliament exercises control over the government through various methods such as **Question Hour, discussions, adjournment motions and no-confidence motions**.

**Adjournment Motion** → A motion used to discuss an urgent matter of public importance by interrupting the normal business of the House.

---

### 3. Prevents Despotism

Executive authority is exercised by a **group of ministers** rather than being concentrated in a single person.

The **Council of Ministers**, under the leadership of the Prime Minister, collectively exercises executive authority. This distribution of power helps prevent the **excessive or dictatorial use of executive power**.

The Executive is also **responsible to Parliament** and must maintain the confidence of the **Lok Sabha**. If the Lok Sabha passes a **no-confidence motion**, the Council of Ministers must resign.

---

### 4. Ready Alternative Government

If the ruling party loses its majority in the **Lok Sabha**, the **Head of State (President)** can invite another party or coalition to form the government.

Thus, a **new government can be formed without immediately holding fresh elections**, as long as another party or coalition can secure the required majority support in the Lok Sabha.

Dr. Jennings described the **Leader of the Opposition as a possible alternative Prime Minister**, because the opposition can form the government if it has majority support in the Lok Sabha.

---

### 5. Wide Representation

In a parliamentary system, the Executive consists of a **group of ministers who are representatives of the people**. Therefore, different **social groups and regions** can be represented in the government.

While selecting ministers, the **Prime Minister can consider regional and social representation**.

⭐ **Key Point:** Parliamentary government allows representation of **different social groups and regions** in the Executive.

---

# Demerits of the Parliamentary System

### 1. Unstable Government

The parliamentary system may lead to an **unstable government**, as there is no guarantee that a government will complete its full tenure.

The Council of Ministers depends on the **majority support of legislators** to remain in office. A **no-confidence motion, political defection, or problems within a multi-party coalition** can make the government unstable.

* **Political defection** → when an elected member changes from one political party to another.

Examples include the governments headed by **Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, V.P. Singh, Chandra Shekhar, H.D. Deve Gowda and I.K. Gujral**, which did not complete their full terms.

⭐ **Key Point:** Dependence on majority support can make parliamentary governments unstable.

---

### 2. No Continuity of Policies

The parliamentary system may make it **difficult to formulate and implement long-term policies** because the government does not have a guaranteed tenure.

If the government changes, the new ruling party may **modify or replace the policies** of the previous government.

⭐ **Key Point:** Frequent changes in government can affect the **continuity of long-term policies**.

---

### 3. Dictatorship of the Cabinet

When the ruling party has a **very large majority in Parliament**, the Cabinet may become very powerful and face fewer parliamentary constraints.

The expression **“dictatorship of the Cabinet”** refers to a situation where the Cabinet becomes **too dominant and exercises excessive power**.

H.J. Laski argued that the parliamentary system can give the Executive an opportunity to **exercise excessive power**. British historian **Ramsay Muir** also criticised the possibility of excessive Cabinet dominance.

The governments of **Indira Gandhi and Rajiv Gandhi are cited as historical examples of this phenomenon**.

⭐ **Key Point:** A very strong parliamentary majority can sometimes lead to **excessive Cabinet dominance**.

---

### 4. Against Separation of Powers

In a parliamentary system, the **Legislature and Executive are closely connected**. Ministers are generally members of Parliament, creating an overlap between the two organs.

The **Cabinet acts as a link between the Executive and Legislature**.

Therefore, the parliamentary system does not follow a **strict separation of powers**. Instead, there is a **fusion of legislative and executive powers**.

**Presidential system → Separation of powers**

**Parliamentary system → Fusion of powers**

---

### 5. Government by Amateurs

The parliamentary system may affect **administrative efficiency** because ministers may not always be experts in the departments they manage.

The Prime Minister generally chooses ministers from among **members of Parliament**. However, a person who is not a member of Parliament can also be appointed as a minister, but must become a member within **six months**.

Moreover, ministers have to spend considerable time on **Parliamentary work, Cabinet meetings and party activities**, along with managing their respective departments.

* **Amateur** → a person without professional or specialised expertise
* **Administrative efficiency** → ability to manage government work effectively
* **Devote** → give time and attention to something

---

# Reasons for Adopting the Parliamentary System

A plea was made in favour of the **US presidential system of government** in the Constituent Assembly. [K.T. Shah]

However, the **founding fathers (constitution-makers)** chose the **British parliamentary system** for the following reasons.

---

### 1) Familiarity with the System

The Constitution-makers were somewhat familiar with the **parliamentary system**, as it had been in operation in India during British rule.

K.M. Munshi argued that India had already experienced some form of **responsible government** for about 30–40 years. He also stated that India’s **constitutional traditions had become parliamentary**.

Thus, after gaining experience with the parliamentary system, there was no need to adopt a **new and unfamiliar system**.

* **Novel** → new and unfamiliar

⭐ **Main Point:** India already had experience with the parliamentary system, so the Constitution-makers preferred to continue with a system they were familiar with.

---

### 2) Preference to More Responsibility

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar pointed out that a democratic Executive should have two qualities: **stability and responsibility**.

* **Stability** → the government should remain in office for a reasonable period.
* **Responsibility** → the government should be accountable to the Legislature.

According to Ambedkar, no system could provide both **stability and responsibility equally**.

The **American presidential system** provides more stability because the President has a fixed term, but less parliamentary responsibility because the Executive does not depend on the continuing confidence of the Legislature.

The **British parliamentary system**, on the other hand, provides more responsibility because the government must maintain the confidence of the Legislature, but less stability.

Therefore, the Draft Constitution preferred the **parliamentary system**, giving greater importance to **responsibility than stability**.

⭐ **Remember:**

**Presidential → More Stability + Less Responsibility**

**Parliamentary → More Responsibility + Less Stability**

---

### 3) Need to Avoid Legislative–Executive Conflicts

The framers of the Constitution wanted to **avoid conflicts between the Legislature and Executive** that could occur in the presidential system followed in the USA.

They believed that a **new democracy like India** could not afford the risk of continuous conflict between the two organs.

Therefore, they preferred a form of government that would promote **cooperation between the Legislature and Executive** and help in the **overall development of the country**.

---

### 4) Nature of Indian Society

India is a **highly diverse and complex society**, with many different **communities, languages, regions, cultures and interests**.

Therefore, the Constitution-makers chose the parliamentary system because it provides greater opportunities to represent **different sections, interests and regions** in the government.

Such representation can help develop a **sense of national unity** among the people and strengthen the feeling of belonging to one nation.

---

# Debate Over the Parliamentary System

Since the **1970s**, there has been debate in India over whether to **continue with the parliamentary system or replace it with the presidential system**.

The issue was examined in detail by the **Swaran Singh Committee**, appointed in **1975**.

The committee concluded that the **parliamentary system was functioning satisfactorily** and therefore **did not recommend replacing it with the presidential system**.

⭐ **Main Point:** **1970s debate → Swaran Singh Committee (1975) → Parliamentary system should continue.**

---

# Distinction Between Indian and British Models

The parliamentary system of government in India is largely based on the **British parliamentary system**. However, it never became a replica of the British system.

### 1. Republican System vs Monarchical System

India has a **republican system** in place of the British **monarchical system**.

The Head of the State in India, that is, the **President, is elected**, while the Head of the State in Britain, that is, the **King or Queen, holds a hereditary position**.

---

### 2. Sovereignty of Parliament vs Limited Parliamentary Powers

The British system is based on the doctrine of **sovereignty of Parliament**, while Parliament is **not supreme in India**.

The powers of the Indian Parliament are limited by the **written Constitution, federal system, judicial review and Fundamental Rights**.

---

### 3. Prime Minister and the Lower House

In Britain, the Prime Minister should be a member of the **Lower House (House of Commons)** of Parliament.

In India, the Prime Minister may be a member of **either House of Parliament**.

---

### 4. Appointment of Ministers

Usually, members of Parliament alone are appointed as ministers in Britain.

In India, a person who is **not a member of Parliament** can also be appointed as a minister, but must become a member of either House within **six months**.

---

### 5. Legal Responsibility of Ministers

Britain has a system of **legal responsibility of ministers**, while India has no such system.

Unlike in Britain, ministers in India are not required to **countersign the official acts of the Head of State**.

* **Countersign** → to sign an official document along with another authority.
* **Legal responsibility** → responsibility recognised by law.

⭐ **Key Point:** **Britain → Countersignature → Legal responsibility**

---

### 6. Shadow Cabinet

A **Shadow Cabinet** is a group formed by the **opposition party** in the British parliamentary system to:

* Keep a check on the ruling government.
* Present alternative policy positions.
* Prepare its members for future ministerial positions.

There is **no such institution in India**.

⭐ **Key Point:** **Opposition → Shadow Cabinet → checks government + presents alternatives + prepares for future government.**
