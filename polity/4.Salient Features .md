# Indian Constitution: Salient Features

The Indian Constitution is **unique in its contents and spirit**.

Though borrowed from almost every Constitution of the world, the Constitution of India has several **salient features** that distinguish it from the Constitutions of other countries.

**Simple meaning:**
India borrowed ideas from many countries, but it **adapted those ideas to India's own needs**.

“It should be noted  that a number of original features of the Constitution (as adopted in 1949) have undergone a substantial change, on account of several amendments.”

The **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** is known as the **“Mini-Constitution”** because it made a very large number of important changes to the Constitution.

In the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), the Supreme Court ruled that although Article 368 gives Parliament the power to amend the Constitution, this power is not unlimited. Parliament cannot alter or destroy the Basic Structure of the Constitution.

## Salient Features of the Constitution

### 1. Lengthiest Written Constitution

The Constitution of India is the **lengthiest written Constitution in the world**. It is comprehensive and detailed.

### Originally — 1949

- **395 Articles**
- **22 Parts**
- **8 Schedules**

### Presently

- About **470 Articles**
- **25 Parts**
- **12 Schedules**

### Various Amendments Carried Out Since 1951

- **About 20 Articles deleted**
- **About 95 Articles added**
- **1 Part deleted — Part 7**
- **4 Parts added — Parts 4A, 9A, 9B and 14A**
- **4 Schedules added — 9th to 12th**

⭐ **Key Point:** The Constitution has become **larger and more detailed through various amendments**.

### Why Is the Indian Constitution So Large?

Four factors have contributed to the **elephantine size** of the Constitution.

#### a) Geographical Factors: Vastness and Diversity

India has differences in:

- **Languages** → Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, etc.
- **Religions** → Hinduism, Islam, Christianity, Sikhism, Buddhism, Jainism, etc.
- **Cultures** → Different festivals, food, dress, traditions and customs across states.
- **Regions** → North India, South India, East India, West India, Northeast India.
- **Social conditions** → Differences in caste, education, literacy, urban-rural conditions, etc.
- **Economic conditions** → Differences in income, employment, agriculture, industries and levels of development.

#### b) Historical Factors

eg : The influence of the Government of India Act of 1935, which was bulky.”

#### c) Single Constitution for the Centre and States

The same Constitution provides the constitutional framework for **both the Union and the States**.

#### d) Dominance of Legal Luminaries in the Constituent Assembly

**Legal luminaries** = highly respected and knowledgeable experts in law.

Many legal experts were members of the Constituent Assembly. Therefore, the Constitution contains many **detailed legal and administrative provisions**.

The Constitution contains not only the fundamental principles of governance, but also detailed administrative provisions.

Many matters that are left to ordinary laws or administrative rules in other modern democratic countries are included in the Indian Constitution itself.

### 2. Drawn from Various Sources

The Constitution of India has borrowed most of its provisions from the **Constitutions of various countries** as well as from the **Government of India Act, 1935**.

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar proudly **acclaimed [stated]** that the Constitution of India was framed after **“ransacking all the known Constitutions of the World.”**

The **structural part** of the Constitution is, to a large extent, derived from the **Government of India Act, 1935**. It was the **most important source** of the Indian Constitution. The **federal scheme, judiciary, Governors, emergency powers, Public Service Commissions and many administrative provisions** were mainly taken from this Act. More than **half of the Constitution’s provisions** are either **similar to or closely based on** the Government of India Act, 1935.

The **philosophical part** of the Constitution derives inspiration from the **American and Irish Constitutions**:

- **Fundamental Rights** → American Constitution
- **Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP)** → Irish Constitution

The **political part** of the Constitution, including the **Cabinet Government and the relationship between the Executive and Legislature**, was largely drawn from the **British Constitution**.

Other provisions were drawn from the Constitutions of **Canada, Australia, Germany, USSR (Russia), France, South Africa, Japan**, and others.

---

### 3. Blend of Rigidity and Flexibility

The Indian Constitution is neither completely rigid nor completely flexible, but a **synthesis of both**.

A **rigid Constitution** requires a special procedure for amendment and cannot be changed in the same manner as ordinary laws, while a **flexible Constitution** can be amended in the same manner as ordinary laws.

In India:

- Some provisions can be amended by a **simple majority of Parliament**.
- Some require a **special majority of Parliament**.
- Some important federal provisions require a **special majority of Parliament + ratification [acceptance/approval] by at least half of the State Legislatures**.

Thus, the Constitution is **flexible enough to adapt to changing circumstances** while being **rigid enough to protect important constitutional provisions from easy alteration**.

⭐ **Note:** Amendments made by **simple majority** do **not come under Article 368**.

---

### 4. Federal System with a Unitary Bias

The Indian Constitution establishes a federal system with a strong unitary bias. Its federal features include the supremacy of the Constitution, division of powers between the Union and States, rigidity of certain constitutional provisions, an independent judiciary and bicameralism. At the same time, it contains several unitary features such as a strong Centre, a single Constitution, single citizenship, an integrated judiciary, appointment of Governors by the Centre, All-India Services and emergency provisions that strengthen the Union. Article 1 describes India as a “Union of States”, which means that the Indian Union is not the result of an agreement among States and that no State has the right to secede from India. Because of its combination of federal and unitary features, K.C. Wheare described India as quasi-federal, while other scholars have described it as bargaining federalism, cooperative federalism and a federation with a centralising tendency.

### 5. Parliamentary Form of Government

India adopted the Parliamentary form of government from the British system rather than the American Presidential system. In the parliamentary system, there is a close relationship and cooperation between the legislature and executive, whereas the presidential system is based more on separation of powers. The parliamentary system is also known as the Westminster Model, Responsible Government or Cabinet Government. It operates at both the Union and State levels. Its main features include the existence of a nominal and real executive, rule by the majority party, collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the legislature, membership of ministers in the legislature, leadership of the Prime Minister or Chief Minister, and the power to dissolve the lower House. However, the Indian system differs from the British system because the Indian Parliament is not sovereign, India is a republic with an elected President, whereas Britain has a hereditary monarch. Because of the important position of the Prime Minister, the Indian system has also been described as Prime Ministerial Government.

### 6. Synthesis of Parliamentary Sovereignty and Judicial Supremacy

The Indian Constitution combines the principles of **Parliamentary Sovereignty** and **Judicial Supremacy**.

- **Parliamentary Sovereignty** → associated with **Britain**, where Parliament has a very high degree of law-making authority.
- **Judicial Supremacy** → associated with the **USA**, where the judiciary has strong power of judicial review.

India follows **neither system completely**.

- Parliament has the power to **make laws** and **amend the Constitution** through its constituent power.
- The Judiciary has the power of **judicial review** to examine laws and declare them unconstitutional if they violate the Constitution.
- Parliament's power to amend the Constitution is subject to **constitutional limitations**, including the **Basic Structure Doctrine**.

⭐ **Key Point:** India maintains a **balance between the powers of Parliament and the Judiciary**.

---

### 7. Integrated and Independent Judiciary

The Indian Constitution establishes a judiciary that is both integrated and independent. It has a single judicial hierarchy consisting of the Supreme Court at the top, High Courts at the State level and subordinate courts below them. Unlike the USA, which has separate federal and State judicial systems, India has one integrated judiciary that enforces both Union and State laws. The Supreme Court performs several important roles: it is the federal court, the highest court of appeal, the guarantor of Fundamental Rights and the guardian of the Constitution. The independence of the judiciary is protected through safeguards such as security of tenure, fixed service conditions, expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, restrictions on discussion of judges' conduct in legislatures except during the removal process, restrictions on practice after retirement, the power to punish for contempt and separation of the judiciary from the executive.

### 8. Fundamental Rights

Fundamental Rights are provided in **Part 3** of the Constitution to protect **individual liberty** and limit **government power**.

There are **six Fundamental Rights**. These rights place limitations on the government and protect citizens against **arbitrary State action**.

They are **justiciable**, meaning they can be enforced through courts. **Article 32** allows a person to approach the Supreme Court for their enforcement.

Fundamental Rights are **not absolute** and are subject to **reasonable restrictions and constitutional limitations**.

⭐ **Key Point:** Fundamental Rights → **Protect individual liberty + Limit government power + Enforceable through courts**.

### 9. Directive Principles of State Policy

The **Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP)** are contained in **Part 4** of the Constitution and were described by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as a novel feature**.

They aim to establish **social and economic democracy** and guide the State towards a **welfare state**.

Unlike Fundamental Rights, DPSPs are **non-justiciable**, meaning they **cannot be directly enforced by courts**. However, the Constitution declares them **fundamental in the governance of the country** and directs the State to apply them while making laws and policies.

⭐ **Key Point:** DPSP → **Welfare State + Social and economic democracy + Non-justiciable**.

### 10. Fundamental Duties

Fundamental Duties were added by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** on the recommendation of the **Swaran Singh Committee**.

They are contained in **Part 4A under Article 51A** and remind citizens of their **responsibilities towards the Constitution, the country, society and fellow citizens**.

The **86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002** added one more duty, making the total **11 Fundamental Duties**.

They are **non-justiciable**, meaning they cannot normally be directly enforced by courts.

⭐ **Key Point:** Fundamental Duties → **Citizens' responsibilities + 11 duties + Non-justiciable**

### 11. Secular State

India is a **secular state**, meaning the Constitution does not establish any particular religion as the **official religion of the State**.

The word **“Secular”** was added to the Preamble by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**, although the secular character of the Constitution existed from the beginning.

Indian secularism emphasises **equal respect and protection for all religions**.

⭐ **Key Point:** Secular State → **No official religion + Equal respect and protection for all religions**.

## Criticism of the Constitution

The Constitution of India has been criticised on the following grounds:

### 1. A Borrowed Constitution

Critics called it a **“borrowed Constitution”** or **“bag of borrowings”** because many provisions were taken from other countries.

**Counter-point:** The Constitution-makers **modified these provisions according to Indian conditions** and did not simply copy them.

### 2. Un-Indian or Anti-Indian Constitution

Critics argued that the Constitution does not sufficiently represent **India's political traditions, culture and spirit** because of its strong foreign influence.

### 3. An Un-Gandhian Constitution

Critics argued that the Constitution did not sufficiently reflect **Gandhian ideals**, especially **village panchayats and decentralisation of power**.

They believed the Constitution should have been built more around **village and district panchayats**.

### 4. Elephantine Size

Critics argued that the Constitution is **too lengthy, bulky and detailed**, containing some unnecessary provisions.

**Meaning:** It contains much more detail than is normally found in a Constitution.

### 5. Paradise of the Lawyers

Critics argued that the Constitution is **too legalistic and complicated**, with detailed legal language and phraseology.

**Meaning:** Its complexity may lead to **more legal disputes and litigation**.

⭐ **Key Point:** The main criticisms were that the Constitution is **borrowed, insufficiently Indian/Gandhian, too lengthy, and too legalistic**.
Too legalistic = too much legal language and detail, making it difficult for ordinary people to understand.
