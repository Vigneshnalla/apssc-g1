# 1. Territorial Extent of Central and State Legislation

The Constitution defines the territorial limits of the legislative powers vested in [granted to] the Centre and the State in the following way.

**Basic question:** Where can Parliament and State Legislatures make laws?

### (i) Parliament

The Parliament can make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India. The territory of India includes the **States, Union Territories, and any other area included in the territory of India at that particular time**.

### (ii) State Legislature

A State Legislature can make laws for the whole or any part of the **State**. The laws made by a State Legislature are not applicable outside the State, except when there is a sufficient nexus [connection] between the State and the object.

### (iii) Extra-Territorial Legislation

The Parliament alone can make extra-territorial legislation [beyond India's territory]. Therefore, certain laws made by Parliament can apply to Indian citizens and their property even when they are outside India.

## Sarkaria Commission

The **Sarkaria Commission**, which examined **Centre–State relations during 1983–1988**, observed that the **rule of federal supremacy**—the priority of Union law in situations of conflict—is a technique to **avoid absurdity, resolve conflicts and ensure harmony between Union and State laws**.

Without Union supremacy in such situations, there could be **interference, disputes, legal confusion and conflicting laws**, making the legal system difficult to understand. It is also necessary to maintain an **integrated legislative policy and uniformity on matters of common Union-State concern**. Otherwise, the federal principle of **“unity in diversity”** could be harmed.

Therefore, **federal supremacy is indispensable for the successful functioning of the federal system**.

**Key idea:** **Federal supremacy → Union law gets priority in appropriate constitutional situations → helps resolve conflicts, maintain uniformity and ensure harmony.**

1. **Taxes on the sale or purchase of goods (other than newspapers) in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.**
2. **Taxes on the consignment of goods in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.**

**Consignment** = sending/dispatching goods from one place to another, usually for sale or delivery.

The **net proceeds** of these taxes do not form a part of the **Consolidated Fund of India**.

They are **assigned to the concerned States**.

C. Levy and Collection of GST in Course of Inter-State Trade or Commerce — Article 269A
'The Goods and Services Tax (GST) on supplies in the course of inter-state trade or commerce are levied and collected by the Centre.But, this tax is divided between the Centre and the States in the manner provided by Parliament on the recommendations of the GST Council.”
> **Parliament can make rules to determine where a supply takes place and whether it is an inter-State supply.**

# D. Taxes Levied and Collected by the Centre but Distributed between the Centre and the States — Article 270

“This category includes all taxes and duties referred to in the Union List except the following:”

### (i) Articles 268, 269 and 269-A

These taxes have their own separate method of distribution, so they are not covered under Article 270.

### (ii) Surcharge under Article 271

A surcharge imposed under Article 271 is fully retained by the Centre.

### (iii) Cess

A cess imposed for a specific purpose is excluded from the divisible pool under Article 270.

### Distribution of Net Tax Revenue

The Finance Commission recommends how the net tax revenue should be divided between the Centre and States, and the President prescribes the distribution based on that recommendation.


# E. Surcharge on Certain Taxes and Duties for Purposes of the Centre — Article 271

“The Parliament can at any time levy the surcharges on taxes and duties referred to in Articles 269 and 270 (mentioned above).”

The proceeds of such surcharges go to the Centre exclusively.

However, the Goods and Services Tax (GST) is exempted from this surcharge.

# Grants-in-Aid to the States

Besides sharing of taxes between the Centre and the States, the Constitution provides for **grants-in-aid to the States from the Central resources**.

**Grant-in-aid =** Financial assistance given by one government to another government.

There are two main types:

1. **Statutory Grants — Article 275**
2. **Discretionary Grants — Article 282**

---

## A. Statutory Grants — Article 275

“Article 275 empowers the Parliament to make grants to the States which are in need of financial assistance and not to every State.”

Different States can receive different amounts of grants.

These sums are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India** every year.

Apart from general financial assistance, the Constitution provides special grants for:

* Improving the welfare of **Scheduled Tribes**, and
* Improving the administration of **Scheduled Areas**, including those in Assam.

“The statutory grants under Article 275 (both general and specific) are given to the States on the recommendation of the Finance Commission.”

---

## B. Discretionary Grants — Article 282

Article 282 allows both the Centre and States to give grants for **any public purpose**, even if that subject is outside their normal law-making powers.

“Under this provision, the Centre makes grants to the States.

They are called **discretionary grants** because the Centre is **not constitutionally required** to provide them. Giving such grants is left to the Centre's discretion.”

These grants have two purposes:

1. **To provide financial help to States for achieving their planned development targets.**
2. **To enable the Centre to coordinate State actions with national development plans.**

---

## C. Other Grants

“The Constitution also provided for a third type of grants-in-aid, but for a temporary period.”

Special grants were provided to **Assam, Bihar, Orissa and West Bengal** as compensation for the loss/replacement of revenue from export duties on **jute and jute products**.

“These grants were to be given for a period of **ten years from the commencement of the Constitution**.”

“These sums were charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India** and were made to the States on the recommendation of the **Finance Commission**.”

# 6. Finance Commission

**Article 280** establishes the **Finance Commission**, which the Constitution describes as a **quasi-judicial body**.

### Important Word

* **Quasi-judicial** → Having certain functions similar to a court or judicial body, although it is not a court.

The Finance Commission is constituted by the **President every fifth year or even earlier**. It makes recommendations to the President on important financial matters concerning the **Centre and States**.

## Functions of the Finance Commission

The Finance Commission recommends:

1. **How the net tax revenue should be divided between the Centre and all States.**

2. **How the States' share should be distributed among the individual States.**

3. **The principles for giving grants-in-aid to States from the Consolidated Fund of India.**

4. **Measures to augment (increase) the resources of the State's Consolidated Fund** so that the State can provide additional financial resources to **Panchayats and Municipalities**, based on the recommendations of the **State Finance Commission**.

### 4. Any Other Matter

The President can refer **any other financial matter** to the Finance Commission for its recommendation if it is necessary for **sound financial management**.

**Means →** The President can ask the Finance Commission to examine any other financial matter if it is important for maintaining proper and healthy financial management.

# 7. Protection of the States' Interest

To protect the **financial interests of States**, the Constitution lays down that the following Bills can be introduced in Parliament **only on the recommendation of the President**.

### 1. Bill Relating to Tax or Duty

A Bill imposing a **new tax/duty** or changing an **existing tax/duty** in which States have a financial interest requires the **President's recommendation**.

### 2. Bill Changing the Meaning of Agricultural Income

A Bill that **changes the legal meaning of “agricultural income”** for the purposes of **Indian income-tax laws** requires the **President's recommendation**.

**Example:**

Income earned from cultivating and producing crops is treated as **agricultural income**.

This definition given by law is the **legal meaning of agricultural income**.

### 3. Bill Changing the Principles of Distribution

A Bill that changes the **principles for distributing money to States** requires the **President's recommendation**.

### 4. Bill Imposing a Surcharge

A Bill imposing a **surcharge on a specified tax or duty for the benefit of the Centre** requires the **President's recommendation**.

---

## Meaning of “Tax or Duty in Which States Are Interested”

A tax or duty is considered one in which **States are interested** when either:

1. The whole or part of its **net proceeds is assigned to any State**, OR
2. The amount payable to a State from the **Consolidated Fund of India** is determined with reference to the **net proceeds of that tax or duty**.

### Net Proceeds

The **CAG (Comptroller and Auditor-General of India)** determines and certifies the **net proceeds** of a tax or duty.

**“His/her certificate is final.”**


# 8. Borrowing by the Centre and the States

### Original Sentence

> “The Constitution makes the following provisions with regard to the borrowing powers of the Centre and the states.”

### a) The Central Government can:

* Borrow within India
* Borrow from outside India
* Give guarantees
