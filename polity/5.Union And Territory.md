# 1. Constitutional Provisions — Articles 1 to 4

“Articles 1 to 4 under Part I of the Constitution deal with the Union and its territory.”

The Constitution's Part I contains Articles 1 to 4.

These Articles explain:

* What India is called
* What territories make up India
* How new States can be admitted or established
* How existing States can be created, changed, merged, divided, etc.

---

# Article 1 — Name and Territory of the Union

“Article 1 describes India, that is Bharat, as a Union of States rather than a ‘Federation of States’.”

Article 1 tells us two important things:

## 1. Name of the country

**India, that is Bharat**

India and Bharat are both names of the same country.

When the Constitution was being made, the members of the Constituent Assembly did not completely agree on the name of India.

Some members preferred the traditional name Bharat.
Others preferred the name India.

Therefore, the Constitution uses both names:

**“India, that is Bharat”**

## 2. Nature of the country

Article 1 describes India as:

**“Union of States”**

and not:

**“Federation of States.”**

India has a federal structure, but the Constitution deliberately uses the expression Union of States.

### Why “Union of States”?

According to Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, the expression “Union of States” was preferred to “Federation of States” for two reasons:

* The Indian Federation is not the result of an agreement among the States, unlike the American Federation.
* The States have no right to secede from the Union.

Therefore:

**India is an indestructible Union of destructible States.**

Meaning:

* The Union cannot be broken by the States.
* The boundaries, areas and names of States can be changed by Parliament.

The country is an integral whole and is divided into different States mainly for administrative purposes.

---

# Territory of India

According to Article 1, the territory of India can be classified into three categories:

## 1. Territories of the States

These are the territories belonging to the 28 States.

## 2. Union Territories

These are also parts of India, but they are not States.

Examples:

* Andaman and Nicobar Islands
* Lakshadweep
* Delhi
* Puducherry

## 3. Territories that may be acquired

Territories that may be acquired by the Government of India in the future.

---

# First Schedule

The Constitution has Schedules along with its Articles.

The First Schedule contains:

* Names of the States
* Names of the Union Territories
* Their territorial extent

At present:

**28 States + 8 Union Territories**

The constitutional rules apply to all states in the same way.

However, the special provisions (under Part XXI) applicable to the States of Maharashtra, Gujarat, Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Goa and Karnataka override the general provisions relating to the states as a class.

As a class → considered as a group.

---

# Fifth and Sixth Schedules

The Fifth and Sixth Schedules contain separate provisions relating to the administration of:

* Scheduled Areas
* Tribal Areas

within certain States.

# States

States are members of India's federal system and share a distribution of powers with the Centre.

# Union Territories

The Union Territories and the acquired territories, on the other hand, are directly administered by the Central government [main authority belonging to government].

---

# Acquisition of Foreign Territory

India can acquire foreign territory through legally recognised methods.

Important terms:

## 1. Cession

Cession means one country or authority transfers territory to another country through an accepted agreement or arrangement.

## 2. Occupation

Occupation means taking possession of territory that was not under the sovereignty of a recognised State.

## 3. Conquest

Conquest means acquiring territory through military victory over another State or ruler.

## 4. Subjugation

Subjugation means bringing a territory or people under control by force.

For example, India acquired several foreign territories such as Dadra and Nagar Haveli; Goa, Daman and Diu; Puducherry; and Sikkim since the commencement of the Constitution.

---

# Article 2

Article 2 gives Parliament two powers:

## 1. Admit a State into India

A State already exists outside the Union, and Parliament allows it to become part of India.

## 2. Establish a new State

Parliament creates a State that did not previously exist.

---

# Article 3

**Article 3 deals with changes in the existing states of the Union of India.**

Parliament can:

* Form a new State by:

  * Separating territory from an existing State
  * Uniting two or more States
  * Uniting parts of States
  * Uniting territory with a part of a State
* Increase the area of a State.
* Diminish the area of a State.
* Alter the boundaries of a State.
* Alter the name of a State.

---

# Article 3 — Procedural Requirements

Article 3 has important procedural requirements.

## Condition 1 — President's recommendation

An Article 3 Bill cannot be introduced in Parliament without the President's recommendation.

### Simple flow:

Proposal to change a State

↓

President recommends

↓

Bill can be introduced in Parliament

## Condition 2 — State Legislature's views

If the Bill affects the:

* Area
* Boundaries
* Name

of a State, the President refers the Bill to the concerned State Legislature to express its views.

The State Legislature is given a specified period to express its opinion.

### Important:

State Legislature's consent is **NOT** required.

The State Legislature can:

* Agree
* Disagree
* Suggest changes

But Parliament is not bound by its views.

After the State Legislature gives its views, the Bill comes back to Parliament.

Parliament may make amendments (changes) to the Bill.

### Question:

Does Parliament have to send the amended Bill back to the State Legislature every time?

**No.**

A fresh reference to the State Legislature is not necessary every time Parliament makes an amendment to the Bill.

---

# Union Territories

In the case of a Union Territory, there is no constitutional requirement to refer the Bill to the concerned legislature for its views.

The Parliament can itself take any action as it deems fit.”

“It is, thus, clear that the Constitution authorises the Parliament to form new states, alter the areas, boundaries or names of the existing states without their consent.”

---

# Article 4

Laws made under:

**Article 2 → admission or establishment of new States**

**Article 3 → formation of new States and changes in area, boundaries or names**

are not treated as constitutional amendments under Article 368.

# Exchange of Territories with Bangladesh

The 100th Constitutional Amendment Act (2015) was enacted to implement an agreement between India and Bangladesh, under which India acquired some territories from Bangladesh and transferred some other territories to Bangladesh.

### Meaning:

India and Bangladesh had an agreement about their international border.

Under this agreement:

* India would receive some territories from Bangladesh.
* India would transfer some territories to Bangladesh.

Because this involved changing Indian territory, a Constitutional Amendment was required.

Therefore, India passed the:

**100th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015**

Under this deal, India transferred 111 enclaves to Bangladesh, while Bangladesh transferred 51 enclaves to India.

**Enclave** → a piece of territory belonging to one country but completely surrounded by another country.

“The India-East Pakistan land boundary was determined as per the Radcliffe Award of 1947.”

In addition, the agreement also dealt with the transfer of territories under adverse possession [areas actually controlled by one country but legally claimed by the other] and the demarcation [official marking] of a 6.1 km stretch of the border that had not yet been properly marked.

“For these three purposes, the amendment modified the provisions relating to the territories of four states (Assam, West Bengal, Meghalaya and Tripura) in the First Schedule of the Constitution.”

---

# Why did India and Bangladesh need this agreement in the first place?

To understand that, we have to go back to 1947.

India and Bangladesh have a common land boundary of approximately 4,096.7 km.

In 1947, British India was divided.

Pakistan was created, and one part of Pakistan was:

**East Pakistan**

The boundary between India and East Pakistan was determined according to the Radcliffe Award.

Some disputes arose from the Radcliffe Award, and an attempt was made to resolve them through the Bagge Award of 1950.

“Another effort was made to settle these disputes by the Nehru-Noon Agreement of 1958.”

However, the proposed division of Berubari Union was challenged before the Supreme Court.

There was a particular territory called Berubari Union in West Bengal.

There was a proposal to divide and transfer part of Berubari Union to Pakistan.

This became a constitutional issue because the question was whether India could transfer its territory to another country through the existing constitutional provisions.

The Supreme Court held that Parliament's power to diminish the area of a State under Article 3 does not include the power to transfer Indian territory to a foreign country.

“Hence, Indian territory can be ceded to a foreign state only by amending the Constitution under Article 368.”

“Consequently, the 9th Constitutional Amendment Act (1960) was enacted to transfer the said territory to Pakistan.”

However, the Supreme Court ruled in 1969 that settling a boundary dispute between India and another country does not require a constitutional amendment.

Such a boundary dispute can be settled through executive action because it does not involve the transfer of Indian territory to a foreign country.

| Situation           | What happens?                                            | Constitutional Amendment?  |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Article 3           | Territory rearranged within India                        | ❌ Not under Article 368    |
| Cession             | India transfers its territory to another country         | ✅ Required                 |
| Boundary settlement | India and another country settle where the boundary lies | ❌ Not necessarily required |

“At the time of independence, India comprised two categories of political units, namely, the British provinces (under the direct rule of British government) and the princely states (under the rule of native princes but subject to the paramountcy of the British Crown).”

The Indian Independence Act (1947) created two independent and separate dominions of India and Pakistan and gave three options to the princely states viz., joining India, joining Pakistan or remaining independent.”

Of the 552 princely states in India, 549 joined India and the remaining 3 (Hyderabad, Junagarh and Kashmir) refused to join India.”

However, in course of time, they were also integrated with India—Hyderabad by means of police action [used military force to bring], Junagarh by means of referendum [was conducted to determine the people's choice] and Kashmir by the Instrument of Accession. [The ruler of Jammu and Kashmir, Maharaja Hari Singh, signed the Instrument of Accession.]

# Dhar Commission and JVP Committee

There had been a demand from different regions, particularly South India, for reorganisation of states on linguistic basis.

Accordingly, in June 1948, the Government of India appointed the Linguistic Provinces Commission under the chairmanship of S.K. Dhar to examine the feasibility of this.

The commission submitted its report in December, 1948, and recommended the reorganisation of states on the basis of administrative convenience rather than linguistic factor.

People were unhappy with the Dhar Commission's recommendation.

and led to the appointment of another Linguistic Provinces Committee by the Congress in December, 1948.

It consisted of:

* Jawaharlal Nehru
* Vallabhbhai Patel
* Pattabhi Sitaramayya [JVP]

“It submitted its report in April, 1949, and formally rejected language as the basis for reorganisation of states.”

However, in October, 1953, the Government of India was forced to create the first linguistic state, known as Andhra, by separating the Telugu-speaking areas from the Madras State.”

This became the first linguistic State.

Potti Sriramulu went on a hunger strike demanding a separate State for Telugu-speaking people.

He continued the hunger strike for:

**56 days**

and died during the agitation.

Once Andhra State was created, people in other parts of India also began demanding:

“If Andhra can be created based on language, why not our language-based State?”

So demands increased throughout India.

“This forced the Government of India to appoint (in December, 1953) a three-member States Reorganisation Commission (SRC) under the chairmanship of Fazl Ali to re-examine the whole question.”

“It submitted its report in September 1955 and broadly accepted language as the basis of reorganisation of states.”

---

# Four factors of the Fazl Ali Commission ⭐

The Commission identified four major factors.

**1. Unity → Language → Economy → Welfare**

“The commission suggested the abolition of the four-fold classification of states and territories under the original Constitution and creation of 16 states and 3 centrally administered territories.”

“By the States Reorganisation Act (1956) and the 7th Constitutional Amendment Act (1956), the distinction between Part A and Part B states was done away with and Part C states were abolished.”

“The States Reorganisation Act (1956) established the new state of Kerala by merging the Travancore-Cochin State with the Malabar District of Madras state and Kasargode of South Canara (Dakshina Kannada).”
