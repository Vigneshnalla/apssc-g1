“Political scientists have classified governments into unitary and federal on the basis of the nature of relations between the national government and the regional governments.”

“By definition, a unitary government is one in which all the powers are vested in the national government and, if regional governments exist, they derive their authority from the national government.”

“In a federal government, the Constitution divides powers between the national and regional governments, and both operate in their respective jurisdictions independently.”

“Britain, France, Japan, China and so on have a unitary model of government, while the US, Switzerland, Australia, Canada, Russia, Brazil, Argentina and so on have a federal model of government.”

“The term ‘federation’ is derived from a Latin word, *foedus*, which means ‘treaty’ or ‘agreement’.”

“Thus, a federation is a new state (political system) which is formed through a treaty or an agreement between the various units.”

“The units of a federation are known by various names, like states (as in the US), cantons (as in Switzerland), provinces (as in Canada), or republics (as in Russia).”

A federation can be formed in two ways:

**Integration**

**Disintegration**

“In the first case, a number of militarily weak or economically backward independent states come together to form a big and strong union, as, for example, the US.”

“In disintegration, a large unitary state becomes a federation by giving autonomy to its provinces.”

**Autonomy → freedom to manage one's own affairs**

The US is the first and oldest federation in the world.

“It was formed in 1787 following the American Revolution (1775–83).”

“It comprises 50 states (originally 13 states) and is taken as the model of federation.”

“The Canadian Federation, comprising 10 provinces (originally 4 provinces), is also quite old—formed in 1867.”

“The framers adopted the federal system due to two main reasons—the large size of the country and its socio-cultural diversity.”

“They realised that the federal system not only ensures the efficient governance of the country but also reconciles (balances) national unity with regional autonomy.”

The Constitution of India does not use the word “Federation”.

“Instead, Article 1 of the Constitution describes India as a ‘Union of States’.”

“According to Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, the phrase ‘Union of States’ has been preferred to ‘Federation of States’ to indicate two things:”

* India was not formed because independent states entered into an agreement, unlike the American federation.
* The states have no right to secede from the federation.

The federation is a union because it is indestructible.

“The Indian federal system is based on the ‘Canadian model’ and not on the ‘American model’.”

The major difference is that the **Canadian model has a very strong Centre**, whereas the **American model gives comparatively greater importance to the states**.

Indian Federation resembles the Canadian Federation in three ways:

* Both formed by way of disintegration
* Preference for the term **Union**
* In its centralising tendency (i.e., vesting more powers in the Centre vis-a-vis the states)

# Features

## Dual Polity

The Constitution establishes a **dual polity [two governments]** consisting of the **Union at the Centre** and the **States at the regional level**.

Each is given **sovereign powers** to be exercised in the field assigned to them respectively by the Constitution.

The **Union Government** deals with matters of **national importance**, like defence, foreign affairs, currency, communication and so on.

The **State Governments** deal with matters of **regional and local importance**, like public order, agriculture, health, local government and so on.

## Written Constitution

“The Constitution is not only a written document but also the lengthiest Constitution in the world.”

“Originally, it contained a Preamble, 395 Articles (divided into 22 Parts) and 8 Schedules.”

“At present, it consists of a Preamble, about 470 Articles (divided into 25 Parts) and 12 Schedules.”

“It specifies the structure, organisation, powers and functions of both the Central and State governments and prescribes the limits within which they must operate.”

Thus, it clearly defines the powers and functions of the Central and State governments and helps avoid misunderstandings and disagreements between the two.

### 3. Division of Powers

The Constitution **divides legislative powers between the Centre and the States** through three lists in the **Seventh Schedule**:

* **Union List** → Centre can make laws.
* **State List** → States can make laws.
* **Concurrent List** → Both Centre and States can make laws. **If there is a conflict, the Central law prevails** (is followed).
* **Residuary Subjects** → Subjects not mentioned in any of the three lists are given to the **Centre**.

### 4. Supremacy of the Constitution

The **Constitution is the supreme (highest) law of the land**.

Neither the **Union Government nor the State Governments** can make laws that **violate the Constitution**.

The laws enacted by the Centre and the States must **follow the provisions of the Constitution**.

Otherwise, such laws can be declared **invalid** by the **Supreme Court or High Courts** through their power of **judicial review**.

The **Legislature, Executive and Judiciary** at both the Centre and State levels must work **within the limits and jurisdictions prescribed by the Constitution**.

## 5. Rigid Constitution

The **division of powers** and **supremacy of the Constitution** can be maintained only if the Constitution is **not easy to amend**.

Therefore, provisions related to the **federal structure**, such as **Centre-State relations and judicial organisation**, cannot be amended by Parliament alone.

Such provisions require:

**Special majority of Parliament + approval of at least half of the State Legislatures.**

⭐ **Key Point:**

**Federal provisions → Special majority of Parliament + approval of at least half of the States.**

## 6. Independent Judiciary

The Constitution establishes an **independent judiciary headed by the Supreme Court** for two main purposes:

1. To protect the **supremacy of the Constitution** through **judicial review**.
2. To **settle disputes between the Centre and the States or between the States**.

The Constitution provides safeguards such as **security of tenure** and **fixed service conditions** to ensure that the **judiciary remains independent of the government**.

* **Security of tenure** → Protection from arbitrary removal from office
* **Judicial review** → Power of courts to examine whether laws are constitutional

## 7. Bicameralism

The Constitution provides for a **bicameral legislature** consisting of two Houses:

* **Upper House → Rajya Sabha**
* **Lower House → Lok Sabha**

The **Rajya Sabha represents the States**, while the **Lok Sabha represents the people of India as a whole**.

The Rajya Sabha helps maintain **federal balance** by protecting the interests of the States against **undue interference by the Centre**.

* **Bicameral** → Having two Houses
* **Federal equilibrium** → Balance between Centre and States
* **Undue** → Excessive or unjustified

⭐ **Key Point:**

**Rajya Sabha → Represents States → Helps maintain federal balance.**

# Unitary Features of the Constitution

Besides the federal features discussed earlier, the Indian Constitution also contains several unitary or non-federal features. These features give the Centre greater power in relation to the States.

## 1. Strong Centre

The division of powers is in favour of the **Centre** and highly inequitable from the **federal angle [point of view]**.

This is because:

* The **Union List** contains more subjects than the State List.
* The **more important subjects** are included in the Union List.
* The Centre has **overriding authority** over the States in certain matters, especially in case of a conflict on the Concurrent List.
* The **residuary powers** are vested in the Centre.

⭐ **Key Point:**

**More powers + important subjects + overriding authority + residuary powers → Strong Centre**

## 2. States Not Indestructible

Indian States cannot **separate or secede from the Union**. The Union of India is therefore indestructible.

Unlike some other federations, **Indian States have no guaranteed right to preserve their existing territory, boundaries or names**.

**Parliament can change the area, boundaries or name of any State** by a **simple majority**.

Hence, India is described as:

> **“An indestructible Union of destructible States.”**

## 3. Single Constitution

“Usually, in a federation, the states have the right to frame their own Constitution separate from that of the Centre.”

India has **one Constitution for both the Union and the States**. States do not have separate Constitutions.

## 4. Single Citizenship

“In spite of a dual polity, the Constitution of India, like that of Canada, adopted the system of single citizenship.”

“There is only Indian Citizenship and no separate state citizenship.”

All citizens, irrespective of the state in which they are born or reside, enjoy the same rights all over the country.

## 5. Flexible Constitution

Many constitutional provisions can be amended by **Parliament alone**, either by a simple majority or special majority, depending on the provision. **State Legislatures do not participate in every amendment.**

## 6. Emergency Provisions

During an emergency, the **powers of the Centre increase greatly** and the federal system operates in a **more unitary manner**.

⭐ **Emergency → Stronger Centre**

## 7. Integrated Judiciary

India has **one integrated judicial system**, with the **Supreme Court at the top, followed by High Courts and subordinate courts**.

## 8. All-India Services

Services such as **IAS, IPS and Indian Forest Service** are common to both the Centre and the States.

## 9. Parliament's Authority Over State List

Under **Article 249**, Parliament can legislate on a **State List subject** if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution declaring it necessary in the **national interest**.

## 10. Governor Appointed by the President

The **Governor is appointed by the President** and holds office during the pleasure of the President, providing a constitutional link between the Centre and the State.

## 11. Integrated Election Machinery

The **Election Commission of India** conducts elections to both **Parliament and State Legislatures**.

## 12. CAG and Integrated Audit Machinery

The **CAG of India** audits the accounts of both the **Union and State Governments**.

## 13. Veto Over State Bills

The **Governor can reserve certain State Bills for the consideration of the President**, who can withhold assent.

⭐ **Important Unitary Features to Remember**

**Strong Centre → Destructible States → Single Constitution → Single Citizenship → Flexible Constitution → Emergency Provisions → Integrated Judiciary → All-India Services → Parliament's power over State List → Governor → Integrated Election Machinery**

# Critical Evaluation of the Federal System — Important Points

The Indian Constitution differs from traditional federal systems like the **USA, Switzerland and Australia** because it contains many **unitary features** and gives greater powers to the Centre. Therefore, there has been a debate among scholars about the **federal nature of the Indian Constitution**.

### 1. K.C. Wheare

* Described the Indian Constitution as **“Quasi-federal.”**
* According to him, India is **more unitary than federal**, with some federal features.

### 2. K. Santhanam

He pointed out the **unitary bias** of the Indian Constitution because:

* The **Centre dominates the financial sphere**, and States depend on Central grants.
* The **Planning Commission** had significant control over the development process of States.
* Therefore, India practically functioned more like a **unitary system**, although constitutionally it was federal.

### 3. Views of Other Scholars

| Scholar                | Description                                      |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **Paul Appleby**       | “Extremely federal”                              |
| **Morris Jones**       | “Bargaining federalism”                          |
| **Ivor Jennings**      | “Federation with a strong centralising tendency” |
| **C.H. Alexandrowicz** | “Sui generis” → unique in character              |
| **Granville Austin**   | “Cooperative federalism”                         |

### 4. Ambedkar's View

* India is **federal** because the Constitution establishes a **dual polity** → Union + States.

* Both the **Union and States derive their authority from the Constitution**.

* States are **not merely agencies of the Union**.

* The Constitution is designed to work as:

  * **Federal in normal circumstances**
  * **More unitary/centralised during exceptional circumstances**

* The legislative and executive powers of the Centre and States are **partitioned by the Constitution itself**, not by an ordinary law of Parliament.

### 5. S.R. Bommai Case (1994)

The Supreme Court held that:

* The Indian Constitution is **federal**.
* **Federalism is a basic feature of the Constitution**.
* States have an **independent constitutional existence**.
* States are **not satellites or agents of the Centre**.
* Within their constitutional sphere, States are **supreme**.
* Exceptional powers of the Centre do not destroy the federal character.

### 6. Indian Federalism — Basic Idea

Indian federalism represents a **compromise between State autonomy and a strong Centre**.

**Normal circumstances → State autonomy + division of powers**

**Exceptional circumstances → Greater power to the Centre**

### 7. Trends Showing Federal Spirit

The federal character of India can be seen through:

* **Territorial disputes** between States, e.g. Maharashtra–Karnataka over Belgaum.
* **River-water disputes**, e.g. Karnataka–Tamil Nadu over Cauvery.
* **Rise of regional parties** and their governments in States such as Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu.
* **Creation of new States** to satisfy regional aspirations, e.g. Mizoram, Jharkhand and Telangana.
* **States demanding more financial grants** from the Centre.
* **States asserting their autonomy** and resisting unnecessary Central interference.
* **Supreme Court limitations on Article 356**, restricting arbitrary use of President's Rule by the Centre.
